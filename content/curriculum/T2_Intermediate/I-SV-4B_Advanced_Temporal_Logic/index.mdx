## Quick Take

- **What it is:** The operators that turn simple SystemVerilog Assertions (SVA) into specifications of real protocols: **repetition** (count events that may be spread out), **sequence composition** (`##0`, `and`, `intersect`, `or`, `first_match`), **interval conditions** (`throughout`, `within`) and **property operators** (`not`, `implies`, `until`, `always`, `s_eventually`, abort operators), together with what *weak* and *strong* mean (IEEE 1800-2023 §16.9, §16.12). Two sub-lessons finish the module: per-attempt **local variables** for data checks, and properties that cross **clock domains**.
- **Why it matters:** Real handshakes wait an unknown number of cycles, must keep a signal steady while they wait, and must answer within a deadline. Written with `##1` chains, those rules are long and wrong in the corner cases; written with these operators they read like the specification. Every protocol checker you write for milestone M6 (AXI and AHB checks) uses them, and so do the bound checkers of I-SV-4C.
- **The Analogy:** An inspector opens a fresh clipboard at every clock tick and ticks boxes as events arrive. Repetition says how many boxes must be ticked and whether they must be next to each other; `throughout` is a condition written across the whole clipboard; `first_match` says "stop at the first box that fits". *Where it breaks:* the inspector only sees a photo taken just before each tick (sampled values), never what happens between ticks; a clipboard still open at closing time is not a failure for the default (weak) forms; and clipboards never share notes, which is exactly what makes local variables work.

**You will be able to:**
1. **Predict** where `[*n]`, `[->n]` and `[=n]` repetitions match on a trace, and **choose** the right one for a handshake rule.
2. **Write** sequences with `##0`, `throughout`, `within`, `and`, `intersect`, `or` and `first_match`, and **predict** their matches on a trace.
3. **Choose** a property operator (`not`, `implies`, `until`, `always`, `s_eventually`, `reject_on`) and **explain** what weak and strong mean for an attempt that is still open when simulation ends.
4. **Debug** a temporal assertion from its log: a bare sequence that fails on idle ticks, a check one tick late, a wait that can never fail, and a later match that hides an error.

### Recap: attempts, sampled values and outcomes

Five words from [I-SV-4A](/curriculum/T2_Intermediate/I-SV-4A_SVA_Fundamentals/index) carry this whole page:

- A **clock tick** is one occurrence of the assertion's clocking event, for example `@(posedge clk)`. A concurrent assertion outside procedural code starts a new **evaluation attempt** at every tick (§16.12.11 calls this the implicit `always`).
- Every value an assertion reads is the **sampled value**: the value the signal had in the Preponed region, just before the tick (§16.5.1). A signal that changes *at* edge 3 is first seen at edge 4. The `disable iff` condition is the exception: it uses current values (§16.12).
- An SVA **sequence** (not a UVM sequence) describes signal values over consecutive ticks. From one start tick it can have zero, one or several **matches**; each match has an **end point**, the tick where it completes. `a ##[1:3] b` can match up to three times for one `a`.
- In `antecedent |-> consequent`, each match of the antecedent starts one check of the consequent on the same tick; `|=>` starts it one tick later (§16.12.7).
- Each attempt ends as a **pass**, a **failure**, a **vacuous success** (the antecedent never matched, so nothing was checked), or **disabled** (the `disable iff` condition was true). An attempt that the end of the trace interrupts is still **open**; the visualizers on this page show it as PENDING, and the strength section below says what the standard calls it.

### Repetition: counting events

**Repetition** says how many times an expression (or a sequence) must match and whether the matches must be back to back (§16.9.2). There are three kinds:

| Operator | Name | Meaning | Ends |
|---|---|---|---|
| `b[*3]` | consecutive | `b` on three consecutive ticks (`b ##1 b ##1 b`) | on the third tick |
| `b[*1:3]` | consecutive, ranged | one to three consecutive ticks | on any of them (up to three matches) |
| `b[->2]` | goto | the second occurrence of `b`, with any gap before each | exactly on the tick of the second `b` |
| `b[=2]` | nonconsecutive | two occurrences, then any number of ticks without `b` | on or after the second `b`, before a third |

Three more consecutive forms appear in real code: `b[*]` means `b[*0:$]`, `b[+]` means `b[*1:$]`, and `b[*0]` is the **empty sequence**, which matches without consuming a tick. The `$` means "finite but unbounded": as many ticks as it takes. Goto and nonconsecutive repetition also take ranges, such as `b[->2:4]` and `b[=1:$]`.

The standard defines the last two through the first (Annex F.3.4.2.3):

- `b[->N]` is `(!b[*0:$] ##1 b)[*N]`: wait through ticks without `b`, take one `b`, N times.
- `b[=N]` is `b[->N] ##1 !b[*0:$]`: the same, then any number of ticks without `b`.

So `start |=> ack[->2] ##1 done` needs `done` on the tick right after the second `ack`, while `start |=> ack[=2] ##1 done` lets quiet ticks pass between the second `ack` and `done`, but no third `ack`.

**What the operand may be.** Consecutive repetition may follow a sequence: `(a ##2 b)[*3]` is legal. Goto and nonconsecutive repetition take only a Boolean expression, with nothing attached: `(a ##1 b)[->2]` and `(b, v = e)[->2]` are illegal (the §16.9.2 syntax). Here `v = e` is a **match item**: an assignment (or a subroutine call) attached to a sequence inside parentheses, which runs when the sequence matches. This matters when you capture data in the Local Variables lesson: `(b[->1], v = e)` is legal, because the `[->1]` sits inside the parentheses.

**What the explorer showed.** `start |=> ack[*2] ##1 done` fails at edge 3, because the consecutive form needs `ack` at edges 2 *and* 3. `ack[->2]` ends at edge 4, the second `ack`, so `done` must be high at edge 5; it is not, and the attempt fails at edge 5. `ack[=2]` may end at edge 5, where `ack` is still low, so `done` at edge 6 completes the match: it passes at edge 6. Now toggle `ack` at edge 5 to 1. `[=2]` fails as well: a third `ack` arrived before `done`.

**Choosing between them.**
- A burst whose beats must come back to back: `[*N]`.
- "The Nth occurrence, and then on the very next tick…": `[->N]`.
- "N occurrences, and the next step may come after any quiet gap": `[=N]`.
- "Wait for the first one": `b[->1]`. It is the workhorse of handshake checks, because it matches on exactly one tick, the first `b`.

### Fusion with ##0

`##1` puts the next element one tick later. `##0` puts it on the **same** tick: the end of the left sequence and the start of the right sequence overlap. The standard calls this an overlapped concatenation (§16.7):

```systemverilog
// snippet: the §16.7 example
(a ##1 b ##1 c) ##0 (d ##1 e ##1 f)
// is the same sequence as
a ##1 b ##1 (c && d) ##1 e ##1 f
```

The everyday use is "when the event finally comes, something else must be true on that same tick": `req |=> ack[->1] ##0 data_ok`. `ack[->1]` ends on the first `ack`, and `##0` checks `data_ok` on that tick.

**Predict, then check.** Before reading the caption, decide which form you would use if the specification says "data is valid in the cycle where ack is high". That is the `##0` form, and on this trace the design under test (DUT) is wrong. The `##1` form would have passed a buggy design, which is why "`##0` waits one cycle" is the most expensive misconception in this module.

**Two rules about empty matches** (§16.9.2.1). An empty match (from `[*0]`) has no tick to fuse with, so `a[*0] ##0 b` never matches, while `1'b1 ##0 b` is just `b`. And `##0` means "same tick" only when both sides use one clock. When the clock changes between them, `##0` waits for the next tick of the new clock unless the two ticks coincide (§16.13.1); the [Multiclock Assertions](/curriculum/T2_Intermediate/I-SV-4B_Advanced_Temporal_Logic/multi-clocking) lesson owns that rule.

### Conditions over an interval: throughout and within

- `busy throughout s` matches when `s` matches and `busy` is true at **every** tick of that match, including its last tick. It is shorthand for `(busy)[*0:$] intersect s` (§16.9.9). The left operand must be an expression, not a sequence.
- `s1 within s2` matches when `s2` matches and `s1` matches somewhere inside it. It is shorthand for `(1[*0:$] ##1 s1 ##1 1[*0:$]) intersect s2` (§16.9.10).

A typical handshake rule combines repetition and an interval: `req |=> busy throughout done[->1]` reads "after a request, busy stays high on every tick until the first done, including the tick where done arrives".

**Try it in the explorer above.** Select **Operator family → Conditions over time**. The trace has `req` at edge 1, `busy` high at edges 2 to 5 and `done` at edge 6. Predict all three results first.

**What it showed.** `req |=> done[->1]` passes at edge 6. `busy throughout done[->1]` fails at edge 6, because `busy` is low on the tick where `done` arrives. `done[->1] within busy[*4]` fails at edge 5: the four-tick `busy` window is edges 2 to 5, and `done` arrives after it closes. Toggle `busy` at edge 6 to 1 and the `throughout` form passes, which is the "last tick counts" rule.

### Combining sequences: and, intersect, or

All three start both operands on the same tick (§16.9.5–§16.9.7):

- `s1 and s2` needs both to match; the result ends when the **later** one ends.
- `s1 intersect s2` needs both to match with the **same** end tick. `throughout` and `within` are built from it.
- `s1 or s2` needs either one; each operand's matches are matches of the result.

When to use each one in a testbench:
- `and`: two independent events must both happen before you continue, in either order. `(wr_done[->1] and rd_done[->1]) ##1 idle` waits for both.
- `intersect`: force two descriptions to cover exactly the same ticks, usually to bound a length: `(busy[*1:8]) intersect (start ##1 done[->1])`.
- `or`: alternative ways to finish, such as `ok[->1] or err[->1]`.

Properties also have `and` and `or` (§16.12.4, §16.12.5). They combine the results of two properties, such as two implications, rather than the matches of two sequences.

### first_match: keep only the earliest match

A range creates several matches. `##[1:3] gnt` from one tick matches at every tick in the window where `gnt` is high. `first_match(seq)` keeps only the match (or matches) of each attempt with the **earliest** end tick and discards the rest (§16.9.8). Whether that changes the result depends on where the sequence sits.

**Before more sequence.** Compare form A, `req |-> ##[1:3] gnt ##1 ack`, with form B, `req |-> first_match(##[1:3] gnt) ##1 ack`, on one trace:

Which is right depends on the specification. If it says "ack follows the *first* grant", form B is the correct check and form A hides a late `ack`.

**In an antecedent.** Each match of the antecedent starts its own consequent check, and every one must pass (§16.12.7). With `req` at edge 1, `gnt` at edges 2 and 3, and `ack` high only at edge 2:

| Edge | `req` | `gnt` | `ack` | Form C: `req ##[1:3] gnt \|-> ack` | Form D: `first_match(req ##[1:3] gnt) \|-> ack` |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 | attempt starts | attempt starts |
| 2 | 0 | 1 | 1 | antecedent match 1 ends; `ack` is 1: check passes | earliest match ends here; `ack` is 1: **passes** |
| 3 | 0 | 1 | 0 | antecedent match 2 ends; `ack` is 0: **fails** | later match discarded |

Form C demands an `ack` after *every* grant in the window; form D after the first one only. Real checks of "the first response after a request" usually need D.

**When it changes nothing.** Around a whole consequent, `first_match` has no effect: one match is enough for a sequence property to hold, so `strong(seq)` and `strong(first_match(seq))` agree, and so do the weak forms (§16.12.2). Do not sprinkle it everywhere; put it in an antecedent or before more sequence.

### Property operators and strength

A **property** is what an assertion asserts: a sequence, an implication, or one of the operators below combined with them (§16.12). Each operator is either **weak** or **strong**. A strong operator requires that its terminating condition actually happen, which includes the clock ticking often enough; a weak one does not (§16.12.15).

| Operator | Strength | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| `not p` | flips | true when `p` is false; it switches the strength of `p` (§16.12.3) | `not strong(a ##1 b)` |
| `p implies q` | — | `p` false, or `q` true, both evaluated from the **same** start tick (§16.12.8) | `mode_a implies always [0:3] !err` |
| `p iff q` | — | both true or both false (§16.12.8) | `en iff out_vld` |
| `p until q` | weak | `p` on every tick before the first `q`; holds if `q` never comes but `p` stays true (§16.12.12) | `busy until done` |
| `p until_with q` | weak | as `until`, and `p` also on the `q` tick | `busy until_with done` |
| `s_until`, `s_until_with` | strong | as above, and `q` must arrive | `busy s_until done` |
| `always p`, `always [m:n] p` | weak | `p` on every (or every listed) tick (§16.12.11) | `always [0:3] !err` |
| `s_always [m:n] p` | strong | every listed tick must exist; the range must be bounded | `s_always [1:4] busy` |
| `eventually [m:n] p` | weak | `p` on some listed tick, or the trace ended first; must be bounded (§16.12.13) | `eventually [1:8] ack` |
| `s_eventually p` | strong | `p` on some current or future tick, unbounded allowed | `s_eventually ack` |
| `nexttime p`, `s_nexttime p` | weak, strong | `p` from the next tick; the weak form holds if there is no next tick (§16.12.10) | `s_nexttime ack` |
| `accept_on(c) p`, `reject_on(c) p` | — | if `c` becomes true during the attempt, the result is true (accept) or false (reject) (§16.12.14) | `reject_on(cancel) done[->1]` |

**Which strength do you get by default?** A plain sequence used as a property is **weak** in `assert property` and `assume property`, and **strong** in `cover property` (§16.12.2). Write `strong(seq)` or `weak(seq)` to choose. So `req |=> ##[1:$] ack` in an assertion never fails because `ack` is missing: the weak sequence has seen no bad state yet.

**What the standard says about an open attempt.** On a finite run, §16.12.21 defines four levels: *holds strongly*, *holds* (no bad state, all obligations met, but a longer run might still break it), *pending* (no bad state, but an obligation is not met yet) and *fails*. An open weak attempt holds; an open strong attempt is pending; neither is a failure. The visualizers on this page use PENDING for any attempt the trace end interrupted, weak or strong.

**`always` in practice.** A concurrent assertion outside an `initial` procedure already starts an attempt at every tick, so `assert property (always p)` there is redundant. The explicit `always` belongs inside a property, as in the standard's `a ##1 b |=> always c`, or in an assertion placed in an `initial` procedure, which runs a single attempt from the first tick (§16.12.11).

### Safety, liveness and unbounded waits

- A **safety** property is one whose every failure happens at a finite time: "busy never drops before done". Simulation can catch all of its failures (§16.12.15).
- A **liveness** property says something good eventually happens: `s_eventually ack`. Its failure "cannot be identified at a finite time" (§16.12.15), so a simulation can never report it as failed while running. It is a natural target for formal tools, which prove a property for every possible input sequence instead of simulating one ([E-INT-1](/curriculum/T4_Expert/E-INT-1_Integrating_UVM_with_Formal_Verification/index)).
- In simulation, turn liveness into safety by **bounding** the wait: `##[1:16] ack` instead of `##[1:$] ack`. The bound comes from the specification (a timeout) or from what you can justify for the design.
- Unbounded ranges also cost simulation time: every attempt stays alive until it resolves, so an unbounded wait started on many ticks keeps many attempts in memory at once. How much that costs is tool-dependent; Push Further has the detail.

### Where these assertions live in a testbench

- **Interfaces.** Protocol rules belong next to the signals, in the interface that bundles them ([F4B](/curriculum/T1_Foundational/F4B_Interfaces_and_Modports/index)), so every testbench that uses the interface gets the checks. The worked example below does exactly that.
- **Bind and checkers.** To attach checks to RTL you may not edit, wrap them in a checker and `bind` it to the design: [I-SV-4C](/curriculum/T2_Intermediate/I-SV-4C_Checkers/index).
- **UVM reporting.** In a UVM environment, failures should be reported through UVM so they count in the report summary; [A-UVM-7](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/A-UVM-7_VIP_Construction/index) shows how VIP interfaces do that.
- **When SVA is the wrong tool.** Data that completes out of order, or that is transformed on the way, is checked by a scoreboard with a reference model ([A-UVM-6](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/A-UVM-6_Scoreboards_and_Reference_Models/index)). SVA is best for cycle-level protocol timing and in-order data paths.
- **Data and clocks.** Comparing a value captured at the request with the value at the response needs a per-attempt local variable (each attempt gets its own copy; attempts never share one), and printing such a value from a match needs a subroutine call attached to the sequence (§16.10, §16.11): [Local Variables in Sequences](/curriculum/T2_Intermediate/I-SV-4B_Advanced_Temporal_Logic/local-variables). Requests and responses on different clocks: [Multiclock Assertions](/curriculum/T2_Intermediate/I-SV-4B_Advanced_Temporal_Logic/multi-clocking).

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| `assert property (req ##1 ack)` | A sequence used as a property must match from every attempt, and it is never vacuous (§16.12.2, §16.14.8), so it fails on every tick where `req` is low | `req \|=> ack` |
| `ack[->1] ##1 data_ok` when data is valid with `ack` | `##1` checks one tick after `ack`; `##0` checks on the `ack` tick (§16.7) | `ack[->1] ##0 data_ok` |
| `[->N]` and `[=N]` treated as the same | `[->N]` ends on the Nth event; `[=N]` may extend over quiet ticks (Annex F.3.4.2.3) | Use `[->N]` when the next step must follow at once |
| `busy throughout done[->1]` with `busy` dropping as `done` arrives | `throughout` includes the last tick of the match (§16.9.9) | Fix the design; if the specification excludes the `done` tick, write `busy until done` (§16.12.12) |
| `req \|=> ##[1:$] ack` as the only response check | Weak and unbounded: a missing `ack` never fails (§16.12.2) | `req \|=> ##[1:16] ack` |
| `req \|=> s_eventually ack` to "make it fail" | Liveness: no failure at a finite time (§16.12.15) | Bound the wait |
| `##[0:7] done ##0 !err` with a range before more sequence | A later match (the next job's `done`) can satisfy the attempt and hide an error | `first_match(##[0:7] done) ##0 !err` |

### Recap

- Repetition counts events: `[*N]` back to back, `[->N]` ending on the Nth, `[=N]` allowing quiet ticks after it.
- `##0` fuses on the same tick in a singly clocked sequence; it never waits a cycle.
- `throughout` covers every tick of the match including the last; `within` places one sequence inside another.
- `and` ends with the later operand, `intersect` needs equal ends, `or` needs either.
- `first_match` matters in antecedents and before more sequence.
- Sequences are weak in assertions and strong in covers; open attempts are not failures; bound every wait you want to fail.

## Make It Work

### Worked example: checking a job engine

The **design under test (DUT)** is a small job engine. A request `req`, sampled while the engine is idle, starts a job of `len` ticks. `busy` is high for `len` ticks and `done` pulses on the last of them. A job of length 0 is illegal; the engine ends it at once and pulses `err` with `done`. The interface `job_if` carries the protocol rules as assertions, and the DUT has a planted bug: on job 2 it drops `busy` one tick early.

```systemverilog
// compile
`timescale 1ns/1ps

// The DUT: req (sampled while idle) starts a job of len ticks.
module job_engine #(parameter bit BUG = 1'b0) (
  input  logic       clk,
  input  logic       rst_n,
  input  logic       req,
  input  logic [2:0] len,
  output logic       busy,
  output logic       done,
  output logic       err
);
  logic [2:0]  left;   // busy ticks still to come after this one
  int unsigned jobs;   // finished jobs; used only to place the planted bug

  always_ff @(posedge clk or negedge rst_n) begin
    if (!rst_n) begin
      busy <= 1'b0;
      done <= 1'b0;
      err  <= 1'b0;
      left <= '0;
      jobs <= 0;
    end else begin
      done <= 1'b0;                        // done and err are one-tick pulses
      err  <= 1'b0;
      if (done) jobs <= jobs + 1;
      if (!busy && req) begin              // accept a job only when idle
        busy <= 1'b1;
        left <= (len == 3'd0) ? 3'd0 : len - 3'd1;
        done <= (len <= 3'd1);             // a one-tick job ends on its first tick
        err  <= (len == 3'd0);             // length 0 is illegal
      end else if (busy && left == 3'd0) begin
        busy <= 1'b0;                      // the done tick has just been sampled
      end else if (busy) begin
        left <= left - 3'd1;
        done <= (left == 3'd1);
        if (BUG && jobs == 1 && left == 3'd1)
          busy <= 1'b0;                    // planted bug: busy drops a tick early
      end
    end
  end
endmodule

// The protocol rules live with the signals, in the interface.
interface job_if (input logic clk);
  logic        rst_n = 1'b0;               // known values before the first edge
  logic        req   = 1'b0;
  logic  [2:0] len   = '0;
  logic        busy, done, err;
  int unsigned n_fail, n_b2b;              // bookkeeping for the summary line

  clocking cb @(posedge clk);              // default skews: input #1step, output #0
    output rst_n, req, len;
    input  busy, done, err;
  endclocking

  // R1: after a request, busy is high on every tick up to and including done.
  a_busy: assert property (@(posedge clk) disable iff (!rst_n)
    req |=> busy throughout done[->1])
    else begin
      n_fail++;
      $error("a_busy failed at %0d ns: busy low before the done tick", $time);
    end

  // R2: a busy pulse lasts 1 to 7 ticks, then busy drops.
  a_len: assert property (@(posedge clk) disable iff (!rst_n)
    $rose(busy) |-> busy[*1:7] ##1 !busy)
    else begin
      n_fail++;
      $error("a_len failed at %0d ns: busy high for 8 ticks", $time);
    end

  // R3: a legal job ends within 8 ticks, and err is low on its done tick.
  a_done_ok: assert property (@(posedge clk) disable iff (!rst_n)
    req && len != 3'd0 |=> first_match(##[0:7] done) ##0 !err)
    else begin
      n_fail++;
      $error("a_done_ok failed at %0d ns: no done in 8 ticks, or err", $time);
    end

  // Coverage: a new request on the tick right after done.
  c_b2b: cover property (@(posedge clk) disable iff (!rst_n) done ##1 req)
    n_b2b++;

  final $display("SVA_SUMMARY failures=%0d back_to_back=%0d", n_fail, n_b2b);
endinterface

module tb_top;
  logic clk = 1'b0;
  always #5 clk = ~clk;                    // rising edges at 5, 15, 25 ns, ...

  job_if jif (.clk(clk));

  job_engine #(.BUG(1'b1)) dut (
    .clk(clk), .rst_n(jif.rst_n), .req(jif.req), .len(jif.len),
    .busy(jif.busy), .done(jif.done), .err(jif.err)
  );

  // One job: a one-tick request through the clocking block, then wait for done.
  task automatic run_job(input logic [2:0] n);
    jif.cb.req <= 1'b1;
    jif.cb.len <= n;
    @(jif.cb);
    jif.cb.req <= 1'b0;
    do @(jif.cb); while (!jif.cb.done);
  endtask

  initial begin
    repeat (2) @(jif.cb);
    jif.cb.rst_n <= 1'b1;                  // the engine leaves reset at 25 ns
    @(jif.cb);
    run_job(3'd3);                         // job 1
    run_job(3'd3);                         // job 2: the planted bug fires
    run_job(3'd1);                         // job 3
    run_job(3'd5);                         // job 4
    repeat (2) @(jif.cb);
    $display("DONE jobs=4 at %0d ns", $time);
    $finish;
  end
endmodule
```

- **`back_to_back=3`.** `done ##1 req` holds after jobs 1, 2 and 3, because `run_job` raises the next request on the tick where it sees `done`. After job 4 nothing follows. A `cover property` counts at most one success per attempt (§16.14.3).

### How to write a temporal assertion

1. **Write the rule in one English sentence**, with the trigger first: "after a request, busy stays high until done, including the done tick".
2. **Choose the trigger as an antecedent** with `|->` or `|=>`, never a bare sequence, unless the rule must hold on every tick.
3. **Pick the waiting operator:** `[->1]` for "the first X", `[=N]` for "N of them, then anything quiet", `##[m:n]` for a window, `throughout` for "while waiting".
4. **Decide `##0` or `##1` deliberately** for every step after a wait: is the next thing on the same tick, or the next one?
5. **Bound every wait** that the specification bounds, and add `first_match` when a range is followed by more sequence.
6. **Add a `cover`** for the trigger and the interesting corners, so a passing assertion is known to have been exercised.
7. **Give each assertion one rule and a message that names it**, so the log tells you which rule broke.

### Debugging temporal assertions

| Symptom in the log | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A failure on almost every tick, including idle ones | A bare sequence asserted as a property (§16.12.2) | Put the trigger in an antecedent |
| The failure time is the tick *after* the event you expected to check | `##1` where `##0` was meant, often after `[->1]` | Use `##0` |
| The failure is at the event tick, and the next tick looks right | `##0` where the specification allows one cycle | Use `##1`; check the specification |
| No failure, although the DUT hung | Weak, unbounded wait such as `##[1:$]` or `[->1]` (§16.12.2) | Bound the wait; look in the simulator's assertion report for attempts still open at the end (tool-dependent) |
| No failure, although one response carried an error | A later match of a range satisfied the attempt | `first_match` before the check |
| Zero failures and a cover count of zero | The antecedent never matched: every attempt was vacuous | Fix the stimulus or the antecedent; always cover the trigger |
| `throughout` fails on the last tick | The condition drops as the event arrives | Decide whether the specification includes that tick |
| Simulation slows down as assertions are added | Many long-lived attempts from unbounded ranges (tool-dependent) | Bound windows; avoid unbounded antecedents |

**Checklist before moving on:**
- [ ] I can predict where `[*N]`, `[->N]` and `[=N]` end on a trace, and I pick `[->N]` when the next step must follow at once.
- [ ] I use `##0` for "on the same tick" and never expect it to wait a cycle.
- [ ] I know that `throughout` includes the last tick of the match.
- [ ] I can say when `and`, `intersect`, `or` and `first_match` change a result.
- [ ] Every response check I write is bounded, and I know that a weak open attempt is not a failure.
- [ ] I can read a failure time in the log and name the operator that caused it.

## Push Further

These items are for working engineers and interview preparation. The core path above does not depend on them.

### Expert: weak, strong and what a finite run can report

- **Defaults.** A sequence property is `weak(seq)` in `assert` and `assume`, and `strong(seq)` in `cover` (§16.12.2). `weak(seq)` holds unless some finite prefix proves the sequence cannot match; `strong(seq)` needs a nonempty match to exist.
- **Strong operators need the clock to tick** (§16.12.15). `s_nexttime a` is false on a trace whose clock stops; `nexttime a` is true there.
- **Four levels on a finite run** (§16.12.21): holds strongly, holds, pending, fails. An open weak attempt *holds*; an open strong attempt is *pending*. The standard does not require a simulator to list open attempts at the end of a run. Many simulators report unfinished or incomplete attempts in their assertion summary, with tool-specific names (tool-dependent: check your simulator's report before relying on it).
- **Ranges.** A weak `eventually [m:n]` and a strong `s_always [m:n]` must be bounded; `s_eventually [m:$]` and `always [m:$]` may be unbounded (§16.12.11, §16.12.13).

### Expert: not switches strength

`not` reverses the result and also the strength of its operand (§16.12.3). The standard's own example: `assert property (@clk not a ##1 b);` treats `a ##1 b` as weak, and a weak sequence holds when the clock stops right after `a`. The negation then fails, although no `b` ever followed. Write `assert property (@clk not strong(a ##1 b));` for "never `a` followed by `b`". The same reasoning explains why "`never` checks" are written with `not strong(...)` in assertion libraries.

### Expert: abort properties versus disable iff

| | `disable iff (c)` | `accept_on(c)` / `reject_on(c)` | `sync_accept_on(c)` / `sync_reject_on(c)` |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where it goes | once, at the top of the assertion's property | anywhere in a property; may nest | anywhere in a property; may nest |
| Value of `c` it reads | current value (§16.12) | sampled value, checked at every time step (§16.12.14) | sampled value, checked only at clock ticks |
| Result when `c` is true during the attempt | attempt **disabled**: neither pass nor fail | **true** (accept) or **false** (reject) | **true** or **false** |
| Typical use | reset | "an abort ends the transfer successfully" or "stop must not occur during the burst" | the same, synchronous to the clock |

If the abort condition and the end of the operand's evaluation fall in the same time step, the abort wins (§16.12.14). `reject_on(stop) put[->2]` is the standard's example; its synchronous form can also be written `!stop throughout put[->2]`.

### Expert: expect in procedural code

`expect (property_spec) action_block` is a blocking procedural statement (§16.17). It starts **one** evaluation at the next clocking event, blocks the calling process until that evaluation passes or fails, then runs the matching action. With no `else` clause, a failure calls `$error`. It is useful in a directed test: `expect (@(posedge clk) ##[1:8] done) else $fatal(1, "job hung");` waits for a job with a built-in timeout, without a separate watchdog process.

### Expert: cover sequence versus cover property

`cover sequence (s)` counts **every** match of each attempt; `cover property (p)` counts at most **one** success per attempt and reports vacuous successes separately (§16.14.3). With ranges this changes the numbers: `cover sequence (req ##[1:3] gnt)` counts three for one `req` followed by three `gnt` ticks, while `cover property` counts one. Choose `cover property` to ask "did this ever happen?" and `cover sequence` to count occurrences.

### Expert: empty matches and degenerate sequences

A sequence that can only match empty, or never at all, is **degenerate** (§16.12.22). The standard forbids degenerate sequences in places where they would give surprising results: a sequence used as a property must not admit an empty match, a `|->` antecedent must be nondegenerate, and attaching a match item (an assignment or a subroutine call) to a sequence that admits an empty match is an error (§16.11). So `(a[*0:2], v = x)` and `assert property (b[*0:3]);` are illegal, while `a[*0] ##0 b` is legal but silently never matches (§16.9.2.1).

### Expert: the cost of long-lived attempts (tool-dependent)

Every attempt that reaches an unbounded range keeps at least one evaluation thread alive until it resolves; a range inside an antecedent can fork one thread per possible match, and an explicit `always` at module level restarts a nested `always` on every tick. Simulators optimise these cases differently, so no general figures apply. Habits that keep large assertion sets fast: bound every window, avoid `[*0:$]` and `##[1:$]` in antecedents, prefer `[->1]` (one match) to `##[1:$]` (a match per tick), and use `first_match` when only the earliest match matters. Measure with your simulator's assertion profiling before optimising.

### Kata: a bounded-latency request/response checker

A request/response interface has `req_vld`, `req_rdy`, `rsp_vld` and `rsp_err`, all sampled at `posedge clk`, with active-low reset `rst_n`. A request is **accepted** on a tick where `req_vld && req_rdy`. The rules:

1. One transfer at a time: after an acceptance, no new request is accepted up to and including the tick where `rsp_vld` arrives.
2. `rsp_vld` arrives 1 to 8 ticks after the acceptance tick.
3. `rsp_err` is low on that response tick (all requests in this bench are legal).

Write the three assertions and one `cover`, then diagnose the four broken versions below **before** opening the solution.

**Diagnose:**
- (a) `assert property (@(posedge clk) req_vld && req_rdy ##[1:8] rsp_vld);` fails on almost every tick.
- (b) `... |=> rsp_vld[->1] ##1 !rsp_err` reports a failure on a response whose `rsp_err` was low.
- (c) `... |=> ##[1:$] rsp_vld` never fails, although the DUT hung after its fifth request.
- (d) `... |=> ##[0:7] rsp_vld ##0 !rsp_err` passes a run in which one response carried `rsp_err = 1`, followed two ticks later by the next transfer's clean response.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution (try the kata first)</summary>

```systemverilog
// snippet: inside the interface that declares clk, rst_n and the four signals
// Rule 1: no second acceptance until (and including) the response tick.
a_one_at_a_time: assert property (@(posedge clk) disable iff (!rst_n)
  req_vld && req_rdy |=> !(req_vld && req_rdy) throughout rsp_vld[->1])
  else $error("a_one_at_a_time: request accepted while one was in flight");

// Rules 2 and 3: the first response within ticks +1..+8, clean on that tick.
a_rsp_ok: assert property (@(posedge clk) disable iff (!rst_n)
  req_vld && req_rdy |=> first_match(##[0:7] rsp_vld) ##0 !rsp_err)
  else $error("a_rsp_ok: no response within 8 ticks, or rsp_err set");

// Coverage: a new acceptance on the tick right after a response.
c_back_to_back: cover property (@(posedge clk) disable iff (!rst_n)
  rsp_vld ##1 (req_vld && req_rdy));
```

Why it works: `|=>` starts the consequent one tick after the acceptance, so `##[0:7]` covers ticks +1 to +8. `first_match` keeps the first response only, and `##0` checks `rsp_err` on that same tick. The `throughout` in rule 1 includes the response tick, matching "up to and including".

The four broken versions:
- (a) A bare sequence: every tick starts an attempt that must see an acceptance, so idle ticks fail (§16.12.2). Put the acceptance in an antecedent.
- (b) `##1` checks `rsp_err` one tick after the response, where it is not valid. Use `##0`.
- (c) A weak, unbounded wait: a missing response is never a failure (§16.12.2). Bound it to 8 ticks.
- (d) Without `first_match`, the range keeps looking after the first response; the next transfer's clean response, still inside the 8-tick window, satisfies the attempt and hides the error.

Next step: the data check ("the response carries the request's data plus one") needs a per-attempt local variable; it is the kata of [Local Variables in Sequences](/curriculum/T2_Intermediate/I-SV-4B_Advanced_Temporal_Logic/local-variables).

</details>

### Debug with the SVA trace lab

The trace lab below evaluates any supported property on an editable trace with the same tested model as the explorer. Use it for three short tasks; predict before each evaluation.

1. **Debug: spot the bug → Bare sequence.** Every `req` gets its `ack` on time, yet the log is full of failures. Name the cause before revealing it (objective 4).
2. **Learn: presets → `req |=> busy throughout done[->1]`.** Predict the results for the attempts at edges 1, 6 and 10. Then switch to **Write your own** (the trace stays) and check `req |=> ##[1:$] done`, then `req |=> ##[1:3] done`. Predict which attempts change.
3. In the same mode, try `req |=> (busy[->1] and done[->1])`, then replace `and` with `intersect` and with `or`.

**What to look for.** In task 2 the preset passes at edge 4 for the attempt at edge 1, fails at edge 9 for the attempt at edge 6 (`busy` drops before any `done`), and leaves the attempt at edge 10 open.
