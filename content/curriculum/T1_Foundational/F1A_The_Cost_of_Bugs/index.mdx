## Quick Take

- **What it is:** The economic case for verification. A **bug** is any difference between what the chip does and what it should do, including a mistake in the specification itself. The later a bug is found, the more finished work has to be redone, so every stage it survives multiplies the cost of fixing it.
- **Why it matters:** A logic bug found after **tape-out**, when the finished layout goes to the factory, can force a **respin**: new photomasks, new wafers and weeks to months of delay. Respins are common: in the 2022 Wilson Research Group study, only about 24% of IC/ASIC projects achieved first-silicon success. Avoiding them is why projects staff about one verification engineer for every design engineer.
- **The Analogy:** Verification is to chip design what crash testing is to car making: expensive, and still far cheaper than a recall. *Where it breaks:* crash tests happen late, on finished prototypes, while verification starts at the first spec review and runs through the whole project. And a recalled car can be repaired in a workshop; fabricated logic can only be worked around or replaced.

**You will be able to:**
1. Explain why fixing the same bug costs roughly ten times more at each later stage, on the commonly cited rule of thumb, and rank the stages (spec review, RTL simulation, full-chip simulation or emulation, post-silicon lab, field) by cost and delay.
2. Choose between a pre-tape-out ECO (engineering change order), a metal-only respin and a full respin for a described bug, and justify the choice by the layers that change, the cost and the schedule.
3. Explain "shift left" and choose the activity that moves a given kind of bug earlier: verification planning, lint, block-level simulation, formal or emulation.
4. Describe the verification lifecycle (spec, plan, testbench, regression, sign-off), who owns each step, and how verification effort compares with design effort, using sourced data.

You need digital logic (gates, flip-flops, clocks, reset, state machines) and any programming language. No hardware description language or verification background is assumed.

## Build Your Mental Model

### Words you will meet

This lesson uses a few industry words before later lessons teach the details. Here is what each one means.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **RTL** (register-transfer level) | The design written as code, such as SystemVerilog modules. Designers edit it and simulators run it. |
| **Simulation** and **testbench** | Running the RTL on a computer. The **testbench** is the code that drives the design's inputs and checks its outputs. |
| **Regression** | The full set of tests, rerun after every change to catch anything the change broke. |
| **Emulation** and **FPGA prototyping** | Running the RTL compiled onto special hardware, fast enough to run real software before the chip exists. |
| **Netlist** and **layout** | Synthesis turns the RTL into a **netlist** of gates. Place-and-route turns the netlist into a **layout**: the geometry of every transistor and wire. |
| **Tape-out** | Sending the finished layout to the fab (the chip factory). |
| **Mask**, **wafer**, **layer** | The fab builds the chip on a silicon **wafer**, one **layer** at a time, and patterns each layer with one or more **photomasks**. The **base layers** make the transistors; the **metal and via layers** above them wire the transistors together. |
| **Respin** | Fabricating a corrected chip, with new masks and new (or held) wafers. |
| **ECO** (engineering change order) | A small, targeted change to the netlist and layout, instead of a full re-implementation. |
| **Lint** | Static checks that read RTL code and flag suspicious constructs, without simulating it. |
| **Formal verification** | Tools that prove a property holds in every reachable state, with no test vectors. |

### The Multi-Million Dollar Question

Why does verification take such a large share of a chip project? In the 2022 Wilson Research Group (WRG) study, IC/ASIC projects had on average about **one verification engineer for every design engineer**, measured as mean peak headcount. In some market segments, such as processors, a 5-to-1 ratio is not unusual. Design engineers themselves spent **49%** of their time on verification.

The reason is that **fabricated logic cannot be patched**. Software ships a fix; a chip's logic is built into its layers. A logic bug found after tape-out usually needs a respin: a **metal-only respin** when the fix fits in rewiring, or a **full respin** when it needs new transistors. If the chip was designed for it, some bugs can instead be worked around in firmware or microcode, or avoided by switching the feature off.

- **Masks:** a full mask set is one of the largest non-recurring engineering (**NRE**) costs of a project, and it grows with each process node. This lesson quotes no dollar figure, because published numbers vary widely and are rarely sourced.
- **Time:** weeks (metal-only) to months (full respin) while new wafers are built and the fix is validated again.
- **Market:** a missed launch window, such as a phone cycle or an automotive program, can cost more than the masks.

The outcome is measurable. In the same study, only about **24%** of IC/ASIC projects achieved first-silicon success, and logic or functional flaws were the leading category of flaws contributing to respins.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **NRE versus recurring cost:** the mask set is paid once per design revision (NRE). The larger damage of a respin is usually the schedule slip, not the masks.

### The design–verification gap

Moore's law, an observation rather than a law of physics, describes transistor counts doubling roughly every two years. Designers get an ever larger canvas, but the ability to check what they build does not grow at the same rate. Before you look at the chart, decide which curve should rise faster, and what would close the gap.

### The cost of finding a bug

The cost of fixing a bug grows with every stage it survives. Make your prediction in the graph first, then step through the stages.

<InteractiveCostOfBugGraph />

The multipliers are a commonly cited **rule of thumb**, not measurements: each later stage costs roughly ten times more than the one before. What drives the jump is how much finished work has to be redone.

| Stage where the bug is found | Rule-of-thumb cost | What has to be redone |
|---|---|---|
| Spec review | ×1 | Reword a requirement and tell the team. Nothing is built yet. |
| RTL simulation (one block) | ×10 | Debug the failing test, edit the RTL, rerun the block regression. Minutes to days. |
| Full-chip simulation or emulation | ×100 | Debug across many blocks and long software runs, then re-verify every block the fix touches. |
| Post-silicon (lab) | ×1,000 | Debug real chips with limited visibility, then a metal-only or full respin, or a firmware workaround. Weeks to months. |
| In the field | ×10,000 | Recalls or replacements, emergency patches that may cost performance, and lost trust. |

Two mistakes everyone makes with this ladder:
- **"Verification is overhead we can cut when the schedule slips."** Cutting checks does not remove bugs. It moves their discovery down this table, where each stage costs about ten times more.
- **Treating the multipliers as data.** They are an order-of-magnitude argument. Real ratios vary by product, node and team: a firmware workaround can make a post-silicon bug cheap, and a safety recall can make a field bug far more expensive.

When a bug is found late, the question after "how do we fix it?" is **escape analysis**: which earlier activity should have caught it, and what do we add to the plan so that kind of bug cannot escape again? It is the debugging skill of this lesson, and Make It Work walks through it.

| Fix | What changes | Relative cost and time | When it is possible |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pre-tape-out ECO** | The design database only. No masks exist yet. | Lowest: engineering time and re-verification | Any bug found before tape-out |
| **Metal-only respin** ("metal spin"): a post-silicon ECO | New masks for some metal and via layers. Spare cells already placed in the design supply the extra gates. | A fraction of a full mask set. Weeks, because the fab can hold partly processed wafers below the metal layers and finish them with the new masks. | The fix fits in rewiring plus the available spare cells |
| **Full (base-layer) respin** | New masks for the transistor layers too, usually most of the mask set | Highest. Months, because new wafers start from the beginning. | The fix needs new transistors or a larger logic change |

Three more tools sit beside the table:
- A **focused ion beam (FIB) edit** cuts and adds wires on a few sample chips in the lab. It confirms that a fix works before you pay for masks; it is not a way to build production parts.
- A **firmware or microcode workaround** changes software so that it avoids the bug or corrects its effect. It only works when the hardware gives software a way in.
- A **defeature bit** (or "chicken bit") is a configuration bit that switches a feature off or forces a safe mode. Designers add them before tape-out for features they consider risky.

**What makes a bug ECO-able:** the fix fits in rewiring plus the spare cells placed before tape-out. It needs no new transistors and no large logic change.

Two pitfalls:
- **"Every silicon fix needs a full respin."** Wrong: metal-only respins exist because many fixes are small rewiring jobs. That is why designs carry spare cells.
- **"A FIB edit fixes the product."** Wrong: it fixes a handful of samples. Production still needs new masks or a workaround.

### Cautionary tales

Bugs that escaped to silicon or to flight are the best teachers. As you page through the cards, ask what check would have caught each one, and when it would have run.

<HallOfShameCarousel />

What each card teaches:
- **Pentium FDIV (1994):** a lookup table that fed every division had wrong entries, and random divides almost never reached them. The cards' sources give Intel's estimate of about 1 in 9 billion random divides. The check that finds this is exhaustive: compare the whole table with its mathematical definition, or prove the divider formally. More random simulation alone did not find it.
- **Ariane 5 Flight 501 (1996):** a software failure, included as a reuse lesson. Code that had flown on Ariane 4 overflowed under Ariane 5's faster trajectory. The missing check: verify reused code again over the new operating range.
- **Spectre and Meltdown (2018):** every instruction returned the architecturally correct value, so functional checks passed. The leak was in what cache timing reveals. The missing checks are security properties about information flow.

The common thread is not one missing technique. In each case **the plan never asked the question that failed**. That is the third classic mistake: **"passing simulation means correct silicon."** A passing regression shows only that the checks you wrote passed for the stimulus you ran.

### Shift left and the verification lifecycle

Verification follows a loop that starts with the specification and ends with a reviewed decision to tape out:

1. **Spec.** Architects write what the chip must do; design engineers and design-verification (DV) engineers review it. Output: a reviewed specification.
2. **Verification plan (vplan).** The DV lead turns every feature into scenarios to run, checks that decide pass or fail, and coverage items that prove the scenarios happened. F1B shows how.
3. **Testbench.** DV engineers build the stimulus, checks and coverage collection the plan calls for.
4. **Regression.** DV engineers run every test on every change; design engineers fix the RTL bugs they find. Output: pass rate, coverage and the bug-discovery trend.
5. **Sign-off.** The DV lead and project leads review the evidence and decide whether the plan is met well enough to tape out.

Two feedback loops keep it honest: every fix reruns the regression, and every escape found later updates the plan.

**Shift left** means moving each activity to the earliest point where it can work, so bugs are found where the cost ladder says they are cheapest:

| Activity | When it runs | What it catches early |
|---|---|---|
| Verification planning | At spec review | Missing, ambiguous or contradictory requirements, at ×1 |
| Lint | As RTL is written | Suspicious code: unintended latches, width mismatches, undriven signals. Dedicated clock- and reset-domain crossing (CDC and RDC) checks look for unsafe crossings. |
| Block-level simulation | As each block works | Functional bugs in one block, with full visibility and fast runs |
| Formal on blocks | As each block works | Corner cases in control logic (arbiters, FIFOs, state machines) that random stimulus rarely reaches |
| Emulation or FPGA prototyping | Before tape-out | Bugs only real software exposes: boot, drivers, performance |

A pitfall: shifting left does not remove the late stages. Full-chip simulation, emulation and post-silicon validation still run, because they find what only they can find, such as interactions between blocks and real software. A second pitfall is the overhead mistake from the cost ladder: when the schedule slips, the plan and regressions are the last things to cut, not the first.

### Who does what

Verification is a team sport with clear hand-offs:

| Role | Owns | Hands over |
|---|---|---|
| Architect | The specification, performance targets | A reviewed spec to design and DV |
| Design engineer | The RTL, block-level checks, bug fixes | RTL releases to DV; fixes for each bug report |
| DV engineer | The vplan, testbench, regressions, coverage | Bug reports to design; evidence for sign-off |
| Formal engineer | Properties and proofs for critical blocks | Proofs, with the assumptions they rely on, or counterexamples |
| Emulation or prototyping engineer | Software runs on the RTL before tape-out | Software-visible bugs and performance data |
| Post-silicon validation engineer | Lab testing of real chips | Escapes, which go back into the plan |

Within DV, the career ladder runs from verification engineer (write tests, build environments, close coverage) through senior engineer (plan complex blocks, integrate verification IP) and verification lead (own the plan and sign-off) to DV architect (methodology and reuse across teams).

Why verification effort rivals design effort:
- **Stimulus alone verifies nothing.** Every scenario needs a check that can fail and coverage that proves it ran.
- **Several levels need their own work:** block, subsystem, full chip and software.
- **Regressions need care.** As the design changes, tests must be updated, debugged and rerun.
- **The last few percent of coverage take a disproportionate share of the effort**, because the remaining scenarios are rare (F1B shows why).

### Recap

- Each later stage costs roughly ten times more to fix the same bug, on the commonly cited rule of thumb. Cutting verification moves bugs to the expensive end.
- After tape-out, a fix is a metal-only respin when it fits in rewiring plus spare cells, and a full respin when it needs new transistors. FIB edits and firmware workarounds buy time; they are not production fixes.
- Escaped bugs share one cause: the plan never asked the question that failed. Escape analysis feeds each one back into the plan.
- Shift left moves planning, lint, block simulation, formal and emulation earlier, and each role owns its part of the loop.

## Make It Work

This orientation lesson has no testbench to build yet. Its main worked example is a set of decisions. A second, small example shows what finding a bug at the cheapest stage looks like in code.

### Worked example: four late bugs, four decisions

| Bug | Found at | Fix | What changes | Rule-of-thumb cost | Typical delay (project-dependent) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. A control register resets to the wrong value; found three weeks before tape-out | Full-chip simulation | Pre-tape-out ECO | The design database; then re-verify and re-check timing | ×100 | Days to weeks; no masks yet |
| 2. A comparator uses "greater than" where the spec says "greater than or equal"; spare cells sit nearby | Post-silicon lab | Metal-only respin, confirmed first on samples with a FIB edit | Some metal and via masks; held wafers are finished | ×1,000 | Weeks |
| 3. The fix needs a new pipeline stage of several hundred flip-flops | Post-silicon lab | Full respin | Base and metal masks; new wafers | ×1,000 or more | Months |
| 4. A customer sees data corruption under a rare traffic mix; a defeature bit can switch the feature off | In the field | Set the defeature bit through a firmware update now; fix the RTL in the next silicon revision | Firmware now, masks later | ×10,000 | Workaround as fast as a firmware release; the real fix waits for new silicon |

Why each decision:
1. Before tape-out no masks exist, so even a late fix is only a database edit. The cost is the debug and re-verification at full-chip level.
2. The fix is a few gates and spare cells are nearby, so only metal and via masks change. A FIB edit on a few samples proves the fix before the masks are bought.
3. New flip-flops need new transistors, which only the base layers can provide.
4. The defeature bit protects customers now. Escape analysis then asks why no test combined that traffic mix, and adds it to the plan.

### Worked example: the same mistake at the cheapest stage

Bug 2 above is a one-character mistake: `>` where the spec says `>=`. Here is what it costs at the RTL stage. You do not need to read SystemVerilog yet (F2A through F2E teach it). The comments say what each part does.

```systemverilog
// compile
`timescale 1ns/1ps

// Spec: alarm must be 1 whenever temp is 100 or more.
module temp_alarm (
  input  logic [7:0] temp,
  output logic       alarm
);
  assign alarm = (temp > 8'd100);    // BUG: the spec says "100 or more", so >=
endmodule

module tb_top;
  logic [7:0] temp;
  logic       alarm;
  int         pass_cnt = 0;
  int         fail_cnt = 0;

  temp_alarm dut (.temp(temp), .alarm(alarm));

  // Apply one value, let the output settle, then compare with the spec.
  task automatic check(input logic [7:0] t);
    logic expected;
    temp = t;
    #1;                                // the output is combinational: no clock needed
    expected = (t >= 8'd100);          // the spec, written independently of the RTL
    if (alarm !== expected) begin      // !== so an unknown (X) output also fails
      fail_cnt++;
      $display("FAIL temp=%0d alarm=%b expected=%b", t, alarm, expected);
    end else begin
      pass_cnt++;
    end
  endtask

  initial begin
    check(8'd99);                      // just below the threshold
    check(8'd100);                     // exactly at it: the boundary the bug breaks
    check(8'd101);                     // just above it
    $display("PASS=%0d FAIL=%0d", pass_cnt, fail_cnt);
    $finish;
  end
endmodule
```

Expected output (traced by hand; not from a simulator run):

```text
FAIL temp=100 alarm=0 expected=1
PASS=2 FAIL=1
```

After the fix, `assign alarm = (temp >= 8'd100);`, the same test prints `PASS=3 FAIL=0`. The whole cycle takes minutes: one test that tries the boundary value, a one-character edit and a rerun. Found on first silicon, the same mistake costs a metal-only respin at best. Notice also what found it: a test that tried the exact boundary, 100. Testing only "typical" values such as 50 and 150 would have passed.

To run it, compile both modules with an IEEE 1800-compliant simulator and make `tb_top` the top module. The exact command is tool-dependent.

### How to triage a late bug

1. **Reproduce and root-cause.** Find the first point where the behaviour diverges from the spec, and confirm the spec says what you think it says.
2. **Ask whether the RTL must change at all.** Can firmware, a defeature bit or a software rule avoid the bug safely?
3. **Before tape-out:** make the ECO in the design database, then rerun the regression and the physical checks.
4. **After tape-out:** if the fix fits in rewiring plus spare cells, plan a metal-only respin and confirm it on samples with a FIB edit; otherwise plan a full respin.
5. **Run escape analysis.** Name the earlier activity that should have caught the bug, and add the missing scenario, check and coverage item to the plan.

**Checklist before moving on:**
- [ ] I can rank spec review, RTL simulation, full-chip simulation or emulation, post-silicon and field by cost and delay, and I call the ×10 per stage a rule of thumb.
- [ ] I can say which masks change for a pre-tape-out ECO, a metal-only respin and a full respin.
- [ ] I can choose the shift-left activity that would catch a given kind of bug earlier.
- [ ] I can say who owns the spec, the RTL, the plan, the testbench, the proofs and the sign-off.
- [ ] I can explain why the boundary value 100 found the comparator bug and a value of 150 would not.

## Push Further

### Expert: Designing for fixability

Teams that expect late bugs design the chip so they can be fixed cheaply:
- **Spare cells:** unused gates spread across the layout before tape-out. They make metal-only fixes possible, at a small cost in area and leakage power.
- **Defeature bits:** each one needs its own verification. Both settings of the bit must be tested, and the "off" mode must itself be safe, or the workaround becomes the next bug.
- **Firmware and microcode patch paths:** they let shipped products change behaviour, but they are also an attack surface and need a security review.
- **The trade-off** is area, power and verification effort before tape-out against cost and schedule risk after it. Processors and other high-volume parts usually invest more.

Source: industry practice. No standard defines spare-cell or defeature strategy; how much a project adds is project- and tool-dependent.

### Expert: Reading the industry data

The WRG 2022 numbers used on this page come from Harry Foster's published analysis:
- about a 1:1 mean peak ratio of verification to design engineers across most market segments, and a 5-to-1 ratio "not unusual" in segments such as processors (Part 8);
- design engineers spending 49% of their time on verification (Part 8);
- about 24% of IC/ASIC projects achieving first-silicon success, against about 30% historically and the lowest value the study had recorded in twenty years, with "logic or functional flaws" the leading category of flaws contributing to respins (Part 12);
- design errors as the leading cause of functional flaws, with changing, incorrect and incomplete specifications "a common theme" (Part 12);
- 980 participants after data cleaning, with a margin of error of ±3.7% at 95% confidence (the study's landing page).

How to read them: the data is self-reported by survey respondents; a mean peak headcount is not the same as total effort; and first-silicon success counts respins for every cause, not only functional bugs. Use the numbers for trends and orders of magnitude, not for a single project's budget.

### Expert: Sign-off is a judgement, not a number

Tape-out sign-off is a review of evidence, not a threshold crossed automatically. A typical review weighs:
- the reviewed verification plan, and whether every feature has scenarios, checks and coverage;
- functional and code coverage, with every exclusion reviewed and justified;
- regression pass rate and stability across seeds;
- a falling bug-discovery rate;
- every open bug, with its severity, its workaround and an explicit risk decision;
- formal results with the assumptions they depend on, and the results of software runs on emulation.

No standard defines sign-off criteria: each project sets them (project-dependent), and how coverage and regression results are collected and merged is tool-dependent. The classic failure is signing off on a number: 100% coverage of an incomplete plan.

### Expert: Shift-left trade-offs

Shifting left has costs of its own:
- Verifying RTL that is still changing wastes effort. Start with plans and lint, then deepen as each block stabilizes.
- Formal on blocks is cheap early, but a proof is only as good as its assumptions (F1B).
- Emulation needs capacity and setup time, FPGA prototypes trade signal visibility for speed, and virtual prototypes let software start before the RTL exists. What each one costs and how much it shows are tool-dependent.

### Estimation kata: four bugs, one ranking

For each scenario, write down the fix you would choose, the stage where the bug was found, its rule-of-thumb cost and the likely delay. Then rank the five stages (spec review, RTL simulation, full-chip simulation or emulation, post-silicon, field) by cost and by delay.

1. During spec review, the DV lead notices that the spec never says what a FIFO does when written while full.
2. Three weeks before tape-out, full-chip simulation finds that an interrupt is cleared one cycle too early. The RTL fix is two lines.
3. On first silicon, a status bit is inverted. An inverter is needed, and spare cells sit next to the register.
4. On first silicon, a cache needs one more way of storage to meet its performance target.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution</summary>

| # | Stage found | Fix | Rule-of-thumb cost | Delay |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Spec review | Clarify the spec (drop the write, or flag an error) and add a "write when full" row to the plan | ×1 | Hours |
| 2 | Full-chip simulation | Pre-tape-out ECO: edit the RTL, rerun the regression and the physical checks | ×100 | Days to weeks |
| 3 | Post-silicon | Metal-only respin using a spare inverter, confirmed first with a FIB edit; a firmware workaround can invert the bit in software meanwhile | ×1,000 | Weeks |
| 4 | Post-silicon | Full respin: more storage means new transistors | ×1,000 or more | Months |

**Ranking by cost:** spec review, RTL simulation, full-chip simulation or emulation, post-silicon, field. **By delay** the order is the same, except that a field bug with a workaround can be contained quickly while its real fix waits for new silicon.
