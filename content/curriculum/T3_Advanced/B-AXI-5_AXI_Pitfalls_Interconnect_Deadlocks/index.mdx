## Quick Take

- **What it is:** The AXI failures that survive a clean per-interface review. A **deadlock** is a set of signals that each wait for another one in the set, so none of them ever changes and the bus stops for good. AXI has three families of them: waits between the channels of one master and one slave, ordering duties inside an **interconnect** (the switch between several masters and several slaves), and incomplete transactions. Next to them sit two classic data bugs, the 4KB-crossing burst and the wrong write strobe (WSTRB).
- **Why it matters:** One illegal wait can hang a whole system on chip, and it often hides until the component meets a different, equally legal partner. A checker that confuses design policy with protocol raises false failures on legal slaves, or stays silent on the master that is actually broken.
- **The Analogy:** Two people at a narrow door, each saying "after you" forever. *Where it breaks:* in AXI only one side of each channel, the destination that raises READY, may wait for the other; the source that raises VALID never may, so the protocol forbids half of the politeness that deadlocks people. And interconnect deadlocks come from ordering rules between transactions, not from two people at one door.

**You will be able to:**
1. Build the wait-for graph of an AXI master and slave and find the closed loop that deadlocks them, such as WVALID waiting for AWREADY while the slave waits for WVALID (IHI0022E §A3.3.1).
2. Classify each wait as a protocol violation (a source waiting for READY, a broken required dependency, a combinational path, an unfinished burst) or a legal but risky destination policy.
3. Explain the ordering duties an interconnect must enforce (§A5.3.1, §A5.3.2, §A5.4.1), trace how write-data ordering deadlocks a two-master, two-slave interconnect, and choose a mitigation.
4. Write VALID and information stability assertions plus a bounded-service check that is labelled as an integration contract, not as an AXI rule.
5. Diagnose 4KB and WSTRB bugs with B-AXI-2's burst and byte-lane equations.

## Build Your Mental Model

**How to read this lesson.** The core path is the wait-for graph, the violation-or-policy table and the progress contract: read those first. The interconnect section, the 4KB and WSTRB diagnosis and the pitfall catalogue are practitioner depth. Make It Work gives you a bench that deadlocks and a triage procedure; Push Further holds the expert layer.

**Words used on this page.** A **source** drives VALID and the information of a channel; the **destination** drives READY ([B-AXI-1](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/B-AXI-1_AXI_Channel_Architecture/index) defines both and the five channels AW, W, B, AR and R). A **handshake** is the rising clock edge where VALID and READY are both HIGH. **Backpressure** is a destination holding READY LOW; the source **stalls** with VALID HIGH. This page uses master and slave as IHI0022E issue E does; later Arm issues say manager and subordinate.

### Wait-for graphs: how to read a deadlock

A **wait** is a design decision of the form "I will not raise signal X until I have seen signal Y". Draw it as an arrow from X to Y and say "X waits for Y". A **wait-for graph** is all the waits of the components on a bus drawn together. If you can follow arrows from a signal and come back to the same signal, you have a **closed loop**: every signal in it waits for the next, so no signal in it can be raised first, and no handshake in it ever completes. That is a deadlock.

A long stall is not a deadlock. A slave that holds AWREADY LOW for 500 cycles while it drains a buffer will raise it eventually; nothing waits back on it. A deadlock is a loop, and a loop never ends by itself.

**Arrow direction.** This page's arrows, like the simulator's, point from the waiting signal to the signal it waits for. The figures in IHI0022E §A3.3.1 point the other way: there a single-headed arrow points to a signal that "can be asserted before or after" the signal at its start, and a double-headed arrow to one that "must be asserted only after" it. So a double-headed arrow is a required wait and a single-headed one a wait the later signal may choose; either way the arrow points from the earlier signal to the later one, the opposite of this page's arrows.

**The classic loop.** IHI0022E §A3.3.1 states the rules "To prevent a deadlock situation": the source's VALID "must not be dependent on" the destination's READY, while a destination "can wait until it detects a VALID signal before it asserts its corresponding READY". Its own Caution gives the example, which B-AXI-1 walks through: a master that waits for AWREADY before driving WVALID deadlocks against a slave that waits for WVALID before asserting AWREADY. **Build the graph in four steps.** Use this on any hang, at one interface or across a whole system.

1. List every signal that is being held: VALIDs that are LOW although the source has something to send, and READYs that are LOW.
2. For each one, read the design (RTL, or the testbench model) and write down what it waits for. Pins alone cannot tell you this.
3. Draw the arrows and look for a closed loop.
4. Classify every arrow in the loop as legal or illegal (next section). A loop with an illegal arrow is a bug in the owner of that arrow. A loop of legal arrows is a system design bug, which is what interconnect deadlocks are.

**Predict, then check.** The simulator below lets you pick each side's waits; it draws the wait-for graph and lists every loop. Before you reveal its answer to "With these waits, what happens to a single write?", predict the outcome of each preset:

- *Classic W/AW deadlock*: the loop above.
- *Legal delayed READY*: the slave's AWREADY waits for AWVALID; the master waits for nothing.
- *Latent violation*: the master's WVALID waits for AWREADY, but this slave's AWREADY waits only for AWVALID.
- *B-channel deadlock*: the master's BREADY waits for BVALID, and the slave's BVALID waits for BREADY.

**Debrief.**
- **Classic:** a loop, so a deadlock. The illegal arrow is the master's; the slave's wait is legal.
- **Legal delayed READY:** no loop and no illegal arrow. Waiting for VALID only adds latency.
- **Latent violation:** the write completes, and the master is still broken. Its illegal wait has no partner waiting back, yet. Put the same master next to the classic slave and the loop closes. **"It works with my slave" is not evidence of compliance**; Push Further shows how to test against the most restrictive legal partner instead.
- **B-channel deadlock:** the same shape on the response channel. The master may wait for BVALID before raising BREADY (§A3.3.1); the slave, which is the source of B, may not wait for BREADY. The read channel works the same way: RREADY may wait for RVALID, and RVALID must not wait for RREADY.

**The two classic mistakes.** First, blaming the slave in the classic loop: a destination waiting for VALID is explicitly allowed. Second, signing off a master because it passed against a slave that is always ready: the latent preset is exactly that master.

### Violation, legal policy, or contract

Every wait you find falls in one of three classes. A **protocol violation** breaks a sentence of IHI0022E, and the owner must fix it. A **legal policy** is a choice the specification allows, which may still be risky in a system. A **contract** is a promise your product makes beyond AXI, such as a latency bound; breaking it is a bug only against that product's specification.

| The wait or behaviour | Owner | Class | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Holds AWVALID, WVALID or ARVALID LOW until it sees a READY | master | **violation** | §A3.2.1, §A3.3.1 |
| Holds BVALID or RVALID LOW until it sees BREADY or RREADY | slave | **violation** | §A3.3.1 |
| Holds AWREADY or WREADY LOW until it has registered AWVALID, WVALID or both | slave | **legal policy** | §A3.3.1 |
| Computes AWREADY combinationally from WVALID in the same cycle | slave | **violation**: the wait is legal, the combinational path is not | §A3.1.1, §A3.2.1 |
| Holds BREADY or RREADY LOW until it sees BVALID or RVALID | master | **legal policy** | §A3.3.1 |
| Raises RVALID before the AR handshake | slave | **violation** (missing required dependency) | §A3.3.1 |
| AXI4: raises BVALID before the AW handshake or before the WLAST handshake | slave | **violation** (missing required dependency) | §A3.3.1 |
| Drops VALID, or changes the information, before the handshake | source | **violation** | §A3.2.1, §A3.2.2 |
| Raises READY, then drops it before any VALID arrives | destination | **legal** | §A3.2.1 |
| Keeps ARREADY LOW for 500 cycles while busy | slave | **legal**; may break a product contract | §A3.2.1 (no bound) |
| AXI3 only: a slave that supports write interleaving stalls W to change its order | slave | **violation** | §A5.3.3 |
| Stops a burst early, or answers an error without completing every transfer | either | **violation** | §A3.4.1, §A3.4.4 |

Three rows deserve a sentence each.

- **Combinational READY.** "On master and slave interfaces there must be no combinatorial paths between input and output signals" (§A3.1.1). So "the slave may wait for WVALID" means it sees WVALID at one edge and raises AWREADY for a later edge. `assign AWREADY = waiting && WVALID;` implements a legal policy illegally.
- **The one READY wait that is not free.** For AXI3 interleaving slaves, §A5.3.3 says: "To avoid possible deadlock, a slave interface that supports write data interleaving must continuously accept interleaved write data." It must never stall W to reorder it. AXI4 removed write interleaving, so this applies only to AXI3 components.
- **Completion is a deadlock rule too.** A master always sends every transfer of a burst and a slave always returns every one, even with an error response (§A3.4.1, §A3.4.4). A component that stops halfway leaves its partner waiting for a WLAST or RLAST that never comes.

**Misconception to drop:** "a slave that waits for WVALID before raising AWREADY violates the protocol". It does not (§A3.3.1). It is the most restrictive legal policy, which is exactly why good verification uses it.

In the simulator, try the custom combinations: give the slave "AWREADY waits for WVALID" and "WREADY waits for AWVALID" with no master waits. The badges show both waits as legal and the write completes: a slave may be that strict. Now add "AWVALID waits for WREADY" on the master and watch the loop close.

### Progress is a contract, not an AXI rule

**What AXI says about time.** IHI0022E gives no number of cycles within which READY must rise: a destination "is permitted to wait for VALID" and may stall for as long as it needs (§A3.2.1). The progress duties it does state are about completion and order, not about time:

- every transfer of a burst completes, even after an error (§A3.4.1, §A3.4.4);
- a peripheral slave given an access it does not support must still complete it in a protocol-compliant way, because "Compliant completion of all transaction types is required to prevent system deadlock" (§A4.1; the interoperability rules in §A10.2.4 repeat it);
- buffered writes must reach their final destination "in a timely manner" (§A4.6), with no number attached;
- quality-of-service (QoS) signals, AxQOS, are a hint: "The protocol does not specify the exact use of the QoS identifier" (§A8.1.1).

**So where does a bound come from?** From your product: an interconnect data sheet, a system QoS specification, a display controller that must refill its buffer every so many cycles. That bound is a **contract**. Write it as a parameter, name its source in a comment, gate it so you can switch it off, and label every failure `[Integration contract]` so nobody reads it as an AXI violation. (AHB is different: its specification expects every subordinate to have a predetermined maximum number of wait states, IHI0033C §5.1.2. Do not carry that habit into AXI.)

**A bounded check is a safety check.** The property below fails at a definite edge, on a finite trace, which is what simulation can check. It says "every stall ends within `MAX_WAIT` edges". It does not prove that the bus always makes progress; that is a **liveness** property, which Push Further covers.

```systemverilog
// snippet: inside a checker module with ACLK, ARESETn and the AXI signals
// [Integration contract] MAX_WAIT comes from the product spec, not from AXI.
property p_bound(valid, ready);
  @(posedge ACLK) disable iff (!ARESETn)
    $rose(valid && !ready) |-> ##[1:MAX_WAIT] ready;
endproperty
a_aw_bound: assert property (p_bound(AWVALID, AWREADY))
  else $error("[Integration contract] AW stalled more than %0d edges", MAX_WAIT);
```

`$rose(valid && !ready)` is true at the first edge of a stall (IEEE 1800-2023 §16.9.3), so each stall starts one attempt and reports at most once. The common form `valid |-> ##[0:N] ready` starts a new attempt at every edge, so one long stall prints a failure on every cycle after the first.

**Misconception to drop:** "bounded READY latency is an AXI rule". It is a contract you choose and source. Where it lives: in the checker module you attach to the interface, next to the protocol assertions but inside its own generate block switched by a parameter, as in Make It Work.

### Interconnect deadlocks

An **interconnect** connects several masters to several slaves; a **crossbar** is one that lets different masters reach different slaves at the same time. Every port of an interconnect is an ordinary AXI interface, so each one can obey §A3.3.1 perfectly while the system still deadlocks. The loop runs through ordering rules between transactions, which [B-AXI-3](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/B-AXI-3_AXI_Ordering_and_IDs/index) introduced.

**The three rules that make write data hard to route.**

1. **A master sends write data in address order.** In AXI4, "All write data must be in the same order as the associated write addresses" (§A5.4.1). A master cannot send the data of its second write before the data of its first.
2. **A slave takes write data in the order it accepted the addresses.** Most slaves "must receive write data in the order in which they receive the addresses", and an interconnect that combines writes from different masters to one slave "must ensure that it forwards the write data in address order", "even if the write transactions have different AWID values, and even if they come from different masters" (§A5.3.2).
3. **W carries no address.** The interconnect learns where a W burst goes only from its AW, so it "must realign the address and write data" before routing (§A3.3, Note).

**Who is at fault?** No interface broke §A3.3.1. The interconnect let each slave accept the addresses in an order that the masters' write data could not follow, and then it had nowhere to park a burst. Its duty in §A5.3.2, to forward write data in address order, became impossible to meet. That is why this is called a system deadlock: the fix is in the interconnect's design, not in a master or a slave.

**Mitigations.** IHI0022E states the ordering duties, not how to meet them; these are common implementation choices, so label them as such in a design review:

- **Order the address acceptances.** Forward a master's AW to a slave only when that master's earlier W bursts have been routed, or only when the interconnect can see that the W order and the AW acceptance order agree.
- **Buffer write data.** With room for one full burst per master port, the interconnect can accept W_a, let M0 send W_b, and break the loop. The hazard is then latent: deeper traffic can need more room.
- **Accept the address together with its data.** Some slaves and bridges take an AW only when they can take its whole W burst (the legal "wait for both" policy at the slave side).

**Other system deadlocks to recognise.**

- **Same-ID reads to different slaves.** "The interconnect must ensure that the read data from a sequence of transactions with the same ARID value targeting different slaves is received by the master in the order in which it issued the addresses" (§A5.3.1). This is an ordering duty, not a deadlock by itself: ignore it and you get an ordering violation. Meeting it usually means stalling the second read until the first returns, or buffering its data; a stall is one more wait arrow, and it can feed a loop together with other blocking paths.
- **Responses that cannot drain.** The specification lets a master wait for BVALID before BREADY, and a slave's response never waits for a new request. A bridge or master that raises BREADY or RREADY only when it has room for its next request can close a loop: the request waits for buffer space, and the space waits for the response. Designing response paths to always drain is a design rule, not a sentence of IHI0022E.
- **A default slave that does not drain W.** When no slave owns an address, the interconnect "must return the DECERR response", typically from a default slave, and "Any component giving a DECERR response must meet" the requirement that all data transfers complete (§A3.4.4). A default slave that answers B without accepting the W burst violates the AXI4 response dependency as well, and leaves the master's W channel stuck behind that burst.
- **A protection block that discards transactions.** It "must provide a protocol-compliant error response to prevent deadlock" (§B1.4.2).

**How verification finds them.** Pin checks at one port cannot. You need crossed traffic: every master writing to every slave with several transactions outstanding, random READY delays on every slave port, and a hang watchdog that prints every pending VALID and READY at every port when no handshake has happened for a long time. Bind a protocol checker at every master port and every slave port of the interconnect, so a violation created inside the interconnect is seen where it happens. Formal tools can search for the loop directly; see Push Further.

### Boundary and strobe bugs, diagnosed with B-AXI-2's model

[B-AXI-2](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/B-AXI-2_AXI_Burst_Math/index) owns the burst equations, the 4KB rule and the byte-lane rules. This section is about diagnosing bugs with them: from a symptom, to the rule, to the check that should have caught it.

**4KB: the symptom.** Part of a burst lands in the wrong place. The interconnect routes a whole burst by its start address, so the transfers past the 4KB boundary still go to the first slave: they alias onto its own addresses, return an error, or corrupt data, depending on that slave, and the slave that owns the next page never sees them. IHI0022E does not define what happens; it simply forbids the burst: "a burst must not cross a 4KB address boundary" (§A3.4.1).

**4KB: the diagnosis.** Only INCR bursts can cross. A legal WRAP stays inside its own window and a FIXED burst repeats one address. Count the last byte from the **aligned** start address:

| Burst | Last byte carried | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| INCR, AxADDR 0x0FE0, AxLEN 7, AxSIZE 3 | 0x0FE0 + 8 x 8 - 1 = 0x101F | **crosses**: illegal |
| WRAP, AxADDR 0x0FF0, AxLEN 15, AxSIZE 2 | wraps inside 0x0FC0 to 0x0FFF | legal |
| FIXED, AxADDR 0x0FFC, AxLEN 15, AxSIZE 2 | every transfer is 0x0FFC to 0x0FFF | legal |
| INCR, AxADDR 0x0FFE, AxLEN 0, AxSIZE 2 | aligned 0x0FFC, so 0x0FFF | legal (0x0FFE + 4 overshoots: wrong formula) |

**4KB: where the check lives.** It is a protocol rule on the AW and AR handshakes, so it belongs in the protocol checker (B-AXI-2's `p_incr_no_4kb` property) or in the monitor's protocol layer, not in the scoreboard. A scoreboard sees only the symptom, a data mismatch at some other address, much later.

**WSTRB: the rule.** The byte lanes a transfer may use are fixed by its address, its size (AxSIZE, 2^AxSIZE bytes per transfer) and the bus width (§A3.4.1). WSTRB marks which of those lanes carry valid data: "A master must ensure that the write strobes are HIGH only for byte lanes that contain valid data" (§A3.4.3). So WSTRB may be any subset of the active lanes, including all zeros, and never a lane outside them.

Work three cases on a 32-bit bus (four lanes, lane 0 is WDATA[7:0]):

| Request | Active lanes (§A3.4.1 equations) | Legal WSTRB | Illegal examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| AxADDR 0x1, AxSIZE 1 (2 bytes), AxLEN 0 | Aligned_Address 0x0, lanes 1 to 1: **lane 1 only** | `4'b0010`, `4'b0000` | `4'b0110`, `4'b0011` |
| AxADDR 0x1, AxSIZE 2 (4 bytes), AxLEN 0 | Aligned_Address 0x0, lanes 1 to 3 | any subset: `4'b0110` writes exactly bytes 1 and 2 | `4'b0011` (lane 0) |
| AxADDR 0x1, AxSIZE 1, AxLEN 1, INCR | transfer 1: lane 1; transfer 2 at 0x2: lanes 2 and 3 | `4'b0010` then `4'b0100` writes exactly bytes 1 and 2 | `4'b0110` on transfer 1 |

Row 1 is the misconception to drop: "2 bytes at 0x1 with AxSIZE = 1 means WSTRB `4'b0110`". With AxSIZE = 1 the transfer is a 2-byte container aligned at 0x0, and the unaligned start leaves only lane 1. To write bytes 1 and 2 in one transfer you need AxSIZE = 2 and a sparse strobe (row 2), or two transfers (row 3).

The other classic bug puts the two bytes at the bottom of the bus with `4'b0011`. Lane 0 is outside the active lanes of row 1, so this is a **protocol violation** a checker can flag on the handshake, with the containment test `(WSTRB & ~active_lanes) != 0`, not only a data corruption that the scoreboard finds later.

Before you look, predict which lanes are active. Then open the strobe panel and toggle `0110`. Change AxSIZE to `4B` and toggle `0110` again. ### Recap

- A deadlock is a closed loop of waits. Find it with a wait-for graph built from the designs, not from the pins.
- A source never waits for READY; a destination may wait for VALID, through a register. Required dependencies (R after AR; in AXI4, B after AW and WLAST) and full burst completion are rules too.
- AXI sets no time bound. A bound is a sourced, labelled integration contract, checked once per stall.
- An interconnect must keep write data in address order at every slave; crossed address acceptance without buffering deadlocks even when every interface is compliant.
- Diagnose 4KB and WSTRB bugs with B-AXI-2's equations: INCR only, from the aligned start; WSTRB must be a subset of the active lanes, which depend on AxSIZE.

## Make It Work

### A deadlock you can run, and fix

The example is one file. `axi_master` is a small write-only master with a `BUGGY` input: when it is 1, the master raises WVALID only after AWREADY. `restrictive_slave` is the **most restrictive legal slave**: it raises AWREADY and WREADY only after it has registered both AWVALID and WVALID, which §A3.3.1 allows. `axi_wr_dep_checker` holds the protocol assertions and one labelled contract, and is attached to the slave with `bind` (IEEE 1800-2023 §23.11), the way a checker joins a real design without editing it. `tb_top` adds a hang watchdog that prints every channel's VALID and READY when nothing has moved for `HANG_LIMIT` edges. All writes are single transfers (AWLEN = 0), so WLAST is always 1.

```systemverilog
// compile
// deadlock_demo.sv: a master that waits for AWREADY meets the most restrictive
// legal slave. No plusarg: the fixed master. +BUGGY_MASTER: the bug.
`timescale 1ns/1ps

// Protocol checks plus one integration contract, for the AW, W and B channels.
module axi_wr_dep_checker #(
  parameter int unsigned MAX_WAIT    = 16,   // contract bound: from the product spec
  parameter bit          CONTRACT_ON = 1'b1
) (
  input logic        ACLK, ARESETn,
  input logic        AWVALID, AWREADY,
  input logic [31:0] AWADDR,
  input logic        WVALID, WREADY,
  input logic [31:0] WDATA,
  input logic [3:0]  WSTRB,
  input logic        WLAST,
  input logic        BVALID, BREADY,
  input logic [1:0]  BRESP
);
  int unsigned fails;   // read by tb_top for the summary line
  task automatic fail(input string rule);
    fails++;
    $error("%s at %0d ns", rule, $time);
  endtask

  // [Protocol A3.2.1] During a stall VALID stays HIGH and the information is stable.
  property p_hold_stable(valid, ready, info);
    @(posedge ACLK) disable iff (!ARESETn)
      valid && !ready |=> valid && $stable(info);
  endproperty
  a_aw_hold: assert property (p_hold_stable(AWVALID, AWREADY, AWADDR))
    else fail("[Protocol A3.2.1] AW dropped or changed during a stall");
  a_w_hold:  assert property (p_hold_stable(WVALID, WREADY, {WDATA, WSTRB, WLAST}))
    else fail("[Protocol A3.2.1] W dropped or changed during a stall");
  a_b_hold:  assert property (p_hold_stable(BVALID, BREADY, BRESP))
    else fail("[Protocol A3.2.1] B dropped or changed during a stall");

  // [Protocol A3.3.1, AXI4] BVALID only after both the AW and the WLAST handshake.
  // Sampled at an edge, the counters hold the handshakes of earlier edges.
  int unsigned aw_cnt, wlast_cnt, b_cnt;
  always_ff @(posedge ACLK or negedge ARESETn)
    if (!ARESETn) begin
      aw_cnt <= 0; wlast_cnt <= 0; b_cnt <= 0;
    end else begin
      if (AWVALID && AWREADY)        aw_cnt    <= aw_cnt + 1;
      if (WVALID && WREADY && WLAST) wlast_cnt <= wlast_cnt + 1;
      if (BVALID && BREADY)          b_cnt     <= b_cnt + 1;
    end
  a_b_dep: assert property (@(posedge ACLK) disable iff (!ARESETn)
    BVALID |-> aw_cnt > b_cnt && wlast_cnt > b_cnt)
    else fail("[Protocol A3.3.1] BVALID before its AW and WLAST handshakes");

  // [Integration contract, not AXI] every stall ends within MAX_WAIT edges.
  // One attempt per stall: $rose fires on the first stalled edge only.
  if (CONTRACT_ON) begin : g_contract
    property p_bound(valid, ready);
      @(posedge ACLK) disable iff (!ARESETn)
        $rose(valid && !ready) |-> ##[1:MAX_WAIT] ready;
    endproperty
    a_aw_bound: assert property (p_bound(AWVALID, AWREADY))
      else fail($sformatf("[Integration contract] AW stalled > %0d edges", MAX_WAIT));
    a_w_bound:  assert property (p_bound(WVALID, WREADY))
      else fail($sformatf("[Integration contract] W stalled > %0d edges", MAX_WAIT));
    a_b_bound:  assert property (p_bound(BVALID, BREADY))
      else fail($sformatf("[Integration contract] B stalled > %0d edges", MAX_WAIT));
  end
endmodule

// Most restrictive legal slave: each READY waits for BOTH VALIDs (A3.3.1),
// one edge later through a register (A3.1.1). Single-transfer writes only.
module restrictive_slave (
  input  logic        ACLK, ARESETn,
  input  logic        AWVALID,
  output logic        AWREADY,
  input  logic [31:0] AWADDR,
  input  logic        WVALID,
  output logic        WREADY,
  input  logic [31:0] WDATA,
  input  logic [3:0]  WSTRB,
  input  logic        WLAST,
  output logic        BVALID,
  input  logic        BREADY,
  output logic [1:0]  BRESP
);
  logic aw_done, w_done;   // handshakes already taken for the current write
  logic both_seen;         // used only inside the flop below: no comb. path out
  assign both_seen = (AWVALID || aw_done) && (WVALID || w_done);

  always_ff @(posedge ACLK or negedge ARESETn)
    if (!ARESETn) begin
      AWREADY <= 1'b0; WREADY  <= 1'b0; BVALID <= 1'b0; BRESP <= 2'b00;
      aw_done <= 1'b0; w_done  <= 1'b0;
    end else begin
      if (AWVALID && AWREADY) begin
        AWREADY <= 1'b0;
        aw_done <= 1'b1;
      end else if (both_seen && !aw_done) AWREADY <= 1'b1;
      if (WVALID && WREADY) begin   // a real slave would store WDATA under WSTRB
        WREADY <= 1'b0;
        w_done <= 1'b1;
      end else if (both_seen && !w_done) WREADY <= 1'b1;
      // AXI4 (A3.3.1): the response follows both handshakes.
      if (BVALID && BREADY) begin
        BVALID  <= 1'b0;
        aw_done <= 1'b0;
        w_done  <= 1'b0;
      end else if (aw_done && w_done && !BVALID) begin
        BVALID <= 1'b1;
        BRESP  <= 2'b00;            // OKAY
      end
    end
endmodule

// Write-only master. BUGGY = 1 makes WVALID wait for AWREADY.
module axi_master #(parameter int unsigned N_WRITES = 4) (
  input  logic        ACLK, ARESETn,
  input  bit          BUGGY,
  output logic        AWVALID,
  input  logic        AWREADY,
  output logic [31:0] AWADDR,
  output logic        WVALID,
  input  logic        WREADY,
  output logic [31:0] WDATA,
  output logic [3:0]  WSTRB,
  output logic        WLAST,
  input  logic        BVALID,
  output logic        BREADY,
  input  logic [1:0]  BRESP,
  output int unsigned writes_done
);
  int unsigned issued;
  logic        b_pend;     // a write is in flight: wait for its response

  // This master can always take a response, so BREADY may stay HIGH (A3.2.2).
  assign BREADY = 1'b1;

  always_ff @(posedge ACLK or negedge ARESETn)
    if (!ARESETn) begin
      AWVALID <= 1'b0; WVALID <= 1'b0; AWADDR <= '0; WDATA <= '0;
      WSTRB   <= '0;   WLAST  <= 1'b0; issued <= 0;  b_pend <= 1'b0;
      writes_done <= 0;
    end else begin
      if (AWVALID && AWREADY) begin
        AWVALID <= 1'b0;
        if (BUGGY) WVALID <= 1'b1;  // BUG: W offered only after AWREADY (A3.3.1)
      end
      if (WVALID && WREADY) WVALID <= 1'b0;
      if (BVALID && BREADY) begin
        b_pend      <= 1'b0;
        writes_done <= writes_done + 1;
        $display("%0d ns: write %0d done, BRESP=%0d", $time, writes_done + 1, BRESP);
      end
      if (!b_pend && issued < N_WRITES) begin   // start the next write
        AWVALID <= 1'b1;
        AWADDR  <= 32'h100 + 4 * issued;
        WDATA   <= 32'hA000 + issued;
        WSTRB   <= 4'hF;
        WLAST   <= 1'b1;                        // AWLEN = 0: one transfer
        WVALID  <= !BUGGY;                      // correct: VALID never waits for READY
        b_pend  <= 1'b1;
        issued  <= issued + 1;
      end
    end
endmodule

module tb_top;
  localparam int unsigned N_WRITES   = 4;
  localparam int unsigned HANG_LIMIT = 32;   // edges with VALID offered, no handshake

  logic        ACLK    = 1'b0;
  logic        ARESETn = 1'b0;
  logic        AWVALID, AWREADY, WVALID, WREADY, WLAST, BVALID, BREADY;
  logic [31:0] AWADDR, WDATA;
  logic [3:0]  WSTRB;
  logic [1:0]  BRESP;
  bit          buggy;
  int unsigned writes_done;

  always #5 ACLK = ~ACLK;

  initial begin
    buggy = $test$plusargs("BUGGY_MASTER");
    repeat (4) @(posedge ACLK);
    ARESETn <= 1'b1;                          // released on a clock edge (A3.1.2)
  end

  axi_master #(.N_WRITES(N_WRITES)) u_mst (.BUGGY(buggy), .*);
  restrictive_slave u_slv (.*);

  // Attach the checker to every restrictive_slave without editing it.
  bind restrictive_slave axi_wr_dep_checker #(.MAX_WAIT(16)) u_chk (.*);

  // Hang watchdog: edges where something is offered and nothing moves.
  // A source that waits without offering anything is invisible here.
  wire hs_any    = (AWVALID && AWREADY) || (WVALID && WREADY) || (BVALID && BREADY);
  wire valid_any = AWVALID || WVALID || BVALID;
  int unsigned stuck;
  always_ff @(posedge ACLK or negedge ARESETn)
    if (!ARESETn)                  stuck <= 0;
    else if (hs_any || !valid_any) stuck <= 0;
    else                           stuck <= stuck + 1;

  initial begin : end_of_test
    int unsigned fails;
    wait (ARESETn === 1'b1);
    fork
      wait (writes_done == N_WRITES);
      wait (stuck == HANG_LIMIT);
    join_any
    fails = u_slv.u_chk.fails;
    if (writes_done != N_WRITES) begin
      fails++;
      $display("HANG at %0d ns: no handshake for %0d edges", $time, HANG_LIMIT);
      $display("  AW: AWVALID=%b AWREADY=%b", AWVALID, AWREADY);
      $display("  W : WVALID=%b WREADY=%b", WVALID, WREADY);
      $display("  B : BVALID=%b BREADY=%b", BVALID, BREADY);
    end
    $display("PASS=%0d FAIL=%0d", writes_done, fails);
    $finish;
  end
endmodule
```

### How to triage a hang

1. **Freeze at the hang.** Let a watchdog or a contract failure stop the run, not a global timeout an hour later.
2. **List the waits.** For every channel at every port, print VALID and READY. A channel with VALID HIGH and READY LOW is stalled; a channel whose source should have something to send but shows VALID LOW is suspicious.
3. **Draw the wait-for graph.** For each held signal, read its owner's design and write down what it waits for.
4. **Classify every arrow** with the table in Build Your Mental Model: violation, legal policy, or contract.
5. **Fix the owner of the illegal arrow.** If every arrow is legal, the loop is a system design problem: an interconnect ordering or buffering issue.
6. **Rerun against the most restrictive legal partner**, like `restrictive_slave`, to prove the fix and to expose latent bugs.
7. **Keep the contract check and the watchdog** in the regression, labelled as contracts.

**Checklist before moving on:**
- [ ] I can draw the wait-for loop of the buggy run and say which arrow is illegal and why.
- [ ] I can explain why no protocol assertion fires in the buggy run although the master is broken.
- [ ] My bound checks are parameterised, gated, sourced in a comment, labelled `[Integration contract]`, and report once per stall.
- [ ] I can tell, for any wait, whether it is a violation, a legal policy or a contract, and cite the section.
- [ ] I can trace the 2x2 write-ordering deadlock and name one mitigation.
- [ ] I can compute the active lanes and the legal WSTRB values for an unaligned narrow write.

## Push Further

### Expert: proving deadlock freedom

A `##[1:N]` bound is a **safety** property: a finite trace can violate it. "Every stall ends eventually" is a **liveness** property; IEEE 1800-2023 writes it with `s_eventually` (§16.12.13), for example `$rose(AWVALID && !AWREADY) |-> s_eventually AWREADY`. A finite simulation can never show that a liveness property holds, only that it has not been satisfied yet when the run stops; how simulators report a strong property still pending at the end of a run is tool-dependent.

Formal tools can prove liveness, but only with **fairness** assumptions about the environment, written as `assume property` (§16.14.2): for example "the master eventually raises BREADY", or the slave's own protocol rules when you verify a master. Without them the tool finds a trivial counterexample, such as a partner that never responds. Real interconnects also need abstraction (fewer outstanding transactions, narrower data) before a proof converges, and liveness engines and their limits differ between tools (tool-dependent). A practical split: bounded contract checks in simulation and formal; liveness proofs on small configurations of the interconnect's ordering and buffering logic.

### Expert: interconnect ordering duties and what they cost

- **Same-ID reads across slaves (§A5.3.1).** The interconnect must return same-ARID read data in issue order even from different slaves. A simple design stalls a second same-ID read to a different slave until the first completes, which costs throughput for masters that reuse one ID. A reorder buffer keeps throughput but costs area and adds a new resource that can fill up, so its full condition must not wait on anything that waits on it.
- **ID manipulation (§A6.3, §A5.3.5).** The interconnect appends master-port bits to IDs so that responses can be routed back; "Any manipulation of the AXI ID values associated with a transaction must ensure that the ordering requirements of the original ID values are maintained". ID width is implementation defined (§A5.3.6): a slave port narrower than the widened IDs forces compression, and compression that merges two IDs merges two ordering streams, which is safe for ordering but can create new stalls; reusing an ID before its response returns breaks routing.
- **Interview probe:** "Where would you put a reorder buffer, and what must never wait for it?"

### Expert: write-data ordering at scale

AXI4 W follows AW order at every master (§A5.4.1), and the interconnect must forward write data to a slave in address order whatever the IDs and masters (§A5.3.2). Write data may also reach an interconnect before its address, which is why the interconnect must realign them before routing (§A3.3, Note). So an interconnect needs, per slave port, the order in which it forwarded AWs, and per master port, the order of that master's W bursts; the deadlock appears exactly when those two orders cannot be merged without storage.

In legacy AXI3 systems, write interleaving lets a slave with an interleaving depth above one take W bursts in a different order using WID, but such a slave "must continuously accept interleaved write data" and "must never stall the acceptance of write data in an attempt to change the order of the write data" (§A5.3.3). AXI4 removed WID and interleaving (§A5.4.1), so AXI3-to-AXI4 bridges must serialise W bursts, which is where crossed-order hazards often reappear.

### Expert: latent violations and the most restrictive legal partner

Section §A3.3.1 gives every destination the right to wait for VALID, so the strictest partner a master can meet is a slave that raises AWREADY and WREADY only after it has seen both AWVALID and WVALID, and ARREADY only after ARVALID. Any master that waits for any READY deadlocks against it. Symmetrically, the strictest partner for a slave is a master that raises BREADY and RREADY only after it sees BVALID and RVALID. This is a methodology derived from §A3.3.1, not a requirement of it.

Use it in three places: as a mode of your responder (the slave model in your verification IP), alongside random READY delays; as the formal environment, by leaving READY unconstrained so the tool explores the strict choice; and in sign-off reviews, where "passes with our always-ready slave model" is not accepted as evidence. A responder that ties READY HIGH can never expose a master that waits on READY.

### Expert: completion as a deadlock-prevention duty

IHI0022E ties completion to deadlock explicitly. Bursts never terminate early (§A3.4.1). "The required number of data transfers must be performed, even if an error is reported" (§A3.4.4): a read of 8 transfers that hits an error still returns 8, each with a response. A DECERR default slave must complete every data transfer too (§A3.4.4). A peripheral slave given an unsupported access may stop working correctly, but "Compliant completion of all transaction types is required to prevent system deadlock" (§A4.1; §A10.2.4 asks for completion "in a protocol-compliant fail-safe manner, to prevent system deadlock"), and a protection mechanism that discards transactions "must provide a protocol-compliant error response to prevent deadlock" (§B1.4.2).

For a testbench this means: error injection never shortens a burst; a responder that injects SLVERR still drives RLAST on the right beat; and a monitor counts beats to WLAST and RLAST regardless of the response. Error responses themselves are legal and are scored, not flagged, by the scoreboard; [B-AXI-6](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/B-AXI-6_AXI_Verification_Performance/index) owns that policy, and [B-AXI-1](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/B-AXI-1_AXI_Channel_Architecture/index) lists the response encodings.

### Kata: violation, policy or legal?

Classify each wait or behaviour as a **violation**, a **legal but risky policy**, or **legal**, and cite the section. Where it applies, check your answer in the deadlock simulator.

1. A master holds WVALID LOW until it sees AWREADY.
2. A slave holds AWREADY LOW until it has registered WVALID.
3. A slave drives `AWREADY = waiting && WVALID` combinationally.
4. A slave asserts BVALID after the WLAST handshake but before the AW handshake (AXI4).
5. A slave keeps ARREADY LOW for 500 cycles while it is busy.
6. A master drops AWVALID after 3 cycles without a handshake.
7. A slave raises ARREADY and drops it again before ARVALID arrives.
8. A slave returns SLVERR on transfer 3 of an 8-transfer read burst and stops.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution</summary>

1. **Violation.** A source must not wait for READY (§A3.2.1, §A3.3.1).
2. **Legal but risky policy.** A destination may wait for VALID (§A3.3.1); it deadlocks only with a master that breaks rule 1.
3. **Violation.** The wait is legal; the combinational input-to-output path is not (§A3.1.1, §A3.2.1).
4. **Violation.** The AXI4 write response dependency requires both the AW and the WLAST handshakes first (§A3.3.1).
5. **Legal.** AXI sets no bound. It may break a product contract, which you check and label separately.
6. **Violation.** Once VALID is asserted it must remain asserted until the handshake (§A3.2.1).
7. **Legal.** "If READY is asserted, it is permitted to deassert READY before VALID is asserted" (§A3.2.1).
8. **Violation.** All 8 transfers must be returned, each with a response (§A3.4.4); bursts never terminate early (§A3.4.1).

</details>

### Debug challenge: draw the loop from a hang log

A 2x2 interconnect hangs. The watchdog prints, at every port (an illustrative log):

```text
HANG: no handshake for 64 edges
  M0: AWVALID=0 | WVALID=1 WREADY=0 (W for AW#1 -> S1) | BVALID=0
  M1: AWVALID=0 | WVALID=1 WREADY=0 (W for AW#1 -> S0) | BVALID=0
  S0: AW order accepted: M0#2, M1#1   | waiting for W of M0#2
  S1: AW order accepted: M1#2, M0#1   | waiting for W of M1#2
```

Here `M0#2` means M0's second write. Draw the wait-for loop. Which component's design choice created it, and what is one mitigation?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The loop: M0#1's data waits for S1, S1 waits for M1#2's data, M1#2's data is queued behind M1#1 (§A5.4.1), M1#1's data waits for S0, S0 waits for M0#2's data (§A5.3.2), and M0#2's data is queued behind M0#1. Every arrow is legal at its own interface. The interconnect created the loop: it let both slaves accept a master's second write before another master's first write, and it has no W buffering to park a burst. Mitigations: forward a master's second AW to a different slave only after its first W burst has been routed, or give each master port enough W buffering to accept a whole burst.

</details>
