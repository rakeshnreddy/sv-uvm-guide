## Quick Take

- **What it is:** Four memory hooks, each mapped to the real rule it stands for. AHB is a **two-stage bridge**: while one truck unloads, the next one states its destination at the gate. AXI is **separate lanes into an interchange**. The five AXI channels are **mail delivery** between a customer and a store. And every AXI channel's VALID/READY handshake (the sender's "my data is valid" signal meeting the receiver's "I am ready" signal) is **a courier at a door**.
- **Why it matters:** The AHB and AXI drivers, monitors and checkers you build in B-AHB and B-AXI are written from a mental picture of the protocol. A wrong picture gives you false failures (a checker that insists write data must follow its address) or a hang (a driver that waits for READY before raising VALID). A correct picture, with its limits known, lets you read any AMBA waveform in seconds.
- **The Analogy:** An AXI write is posting a parcel. You hand the store a shipping label (the write address, AW) and the box (the write data, W) at two separate counters, and the store mails you a receipt (the write response, B). A read is placing an order (AR) and receiving the goods with a packing slip (R). *Where it breaks:* the box may reach the store before its label, and the receipt waits for both; on a read every item in the shipment carries its own slip, not one slip per order; and a customer may keep many orders open at once. Each section below states where its picture stops being true.

**You will be able to:**
1. Explain AHB's pipelined bus and AXI's independent channels with an analogy each, and state where each analogy breaks.
2. Recall the five AXI channels, the direction of each payload, and who drives VALID and READY on each.
3. Predict whether a described VALID/READY handshake or channel order is legal: the source never waits for READY; VALID and its payload hold until the transfer; R comes only after AR; in AXI4, B comes only after AW and the last W beat.
4. Predict which AHB phases a wait state holds: the current transfer's data phase and the next transfer's address phase.

## Build Your Mental Model

**How to read this lesson.** The core path is the four pictures: the AHB bridge, the AXI interchange, the five channels, and the courier at the door. Each comes with a waveform or diagram and a list of where it breaks. Practitioner depth follows: the misconceptions, how the hooks become driver, monitor and checker code, and how to debug with them. B-AHB-1 and B-AXI-1 own the normative rules; this lesson builds the intuition they rely on.

**Words you need first.** A **bus protocol** is a set of rules for how two hardware blocks exchange data over wires. The block that starts each exchange is the **master**, and the block that answers is the **slave**; the specifications this lesson cites (Arm IHI0022E for AXI, IHI0033B.b for AHB) use these terms, and later Arm issues call them manager and subordinate. An **interconnect** is the block in the middle that routes masters to slaves. A **transfer** is one item of information moving across the interface, and a **burst** is a run of transfers that share one request; each transfer of a burst is a **beat**.

### AHB: one road, a two-stage bridge

**AHB** (Advanced High-performance Bus) defines a bus where every transfer has two **phases**, each lasting at least one cycle of the bus clock HCLK, with everything sampled on its rising edge (IHI0033B.b §1.1, §1.3):

- the **address phase**, one cycle in which the master drives the address (HADDR) and control signals, such as HWRITE (read or write) and HTRANS (whether this is a real transfer: NONSEQ for a single transfer or the first beat of a burst, IDLE for none; §3.2, Table 3-1);
- the **data phase**, one or more cycles in which the write data (HWDATA) or read data (HRDATA) moves.

The key fact is that the phases **overlap**. The address phase of any transfer happens during the data phase of the previous one (§3.1). That overlap is what *pipelined* means here.

**The hook: a two-stage bridge.** Picture a bridge with a gate at the near end and an unloading bay at the far end. While truck A unloads at the bay (A's data phase), truck B stands at the gate stating its destination (B's address phase). On the next clock edge both move up one stage. A **gatekeeper** reads each destination and opens the right factory's door: this is the **decoder**, which turns the address into a select signal (HSELx) for one slave. A **return window** passes each factory's cargo back to the master: this is the **multiplexor**, which routes the selected slave's HRDATA and its transfer response (HRESP) back (§1.1, §1.1.3).

**Waiting.** A slave that needs more time drives its **HREADYOUT** output low; the interconnect combines every slave's HREADYOUT into one **HREADY** signal that all components see (§3.1). An edge where HREADY is low is a **wait state**. A slave can only stretch the data phase; it cannot ask for a longer address phase (§1.3). Because the next address waits at the gate while the current truck is still unloading, a wait holds both: "extending the data phase of the transfer to address B has the effect of extending the address phase of the transfer to address C" (IHI0033B.b §3.1, Figure 3-5).

**Where the bridge breaks.**
- **Two trucks are on the bridge at once,** one per stage. A bridge you picture as "one truck at a time" misses the pipeline entirely (§3.1).
- **There is no traffic cop in the protocol.** An AHB master interface has no request or grant signals (§2.2); it behaves as if it owned the bus. A system with several masters needs an interconnect that arbitrates and routes, and the AHB specification leaves its design (single-layer or multi-layer) out of scope (§1.1, §1.1.3, §4.1). Whether a slow slave also stalls other masters therefore depends on that interconnect, not on the protocol; with a multi-layer interconnect it usually does not (engineering practice, not stated in IHI0033B.b).
- **Cargo flows both ways on separate roads.** Write data goes out on HWDATA, which every slave sees; read data comes back on HRDATA through the multiplexor (§1.1, §1.3).
- **A burst does not skip the gate.** Each beat of an AHB burst still has its own address phase: NONSEQ for the first beat, then SEQ with the next address (§3.2, Table 3-1).

AHB is not "slow": the specification presents it as a bus for high-performance designs, and keeps low-bandwidth peripherals on APB (§1.1). Its limit is that one master's transfers go through one pipeline, one address and one data phase at a time.

### AXI: separate lanes into an interchange

**AXI** (Advanced eXtensible Interface) takes the AHB idea of separate address and data a step further. Instead of two phases on one bus, AXI defines **five independent channels**, each a one-way set of wires with its own handshake (IHI0022E §A1.3). A master can issue several addresses before any data returns, can have **multiple outstanding transactions** (requests started but not yet finished), and can receive them out of order when it tags them with different **IDs** (tag numbers the master attaches to each transaction; §A1.3, §A5.3).

**The hook: lanes into an interchange.** Each master has its own on-ramp to an interchange (the interconnect), and the interchange routes each vehicle to its factory. Two masters can send at the same moment, and inside each on-ramp, addresses, write data, responses and read data travel in separate lanes.

**Where the interchange breaks.**
- **The interchange may share roads inside.** AXI defines one interface for master-to-interconnect, interconnect-to-slave and master-to-slave links. Real interconnects use shared address and data buses, a shared address bus with several data buses, or a multilayer design (§A1.3.2). "Point to point" describes the interface, not every wire inside the interconnect.
- **The lanes are not fully independent.** A write response waits for its write data, read data waits for its address, and handshakes follow the dependency rules (§A3.3, §A3.3.1). The next two sections show exactly which dependencies exist.
- **Some traffic keeps its order.** Transactions from one master with the same ID, in the same direction (all reads or all writes), return their responses in issue order (§A5.3), and in AXI4 write data arrives in the order of the write addresses (§A5.4.1).

### The five channels: who sends what, who drives VALID and READY

Every channel carries a **payload** (the information signals) plus two handshake signals. The **source** of the payload drives **VALID** ("the information on the wires is real"); the **destination** drives **READY** ("I can take it") (IHI0022E §A3.2.1, Table A3-1). One hook covers all five: **VALID travels with the payload; READY travels against it.**

| Channel | Payload (main signals) | Payload direction | VALID driven by | READY driven by | Last-beat signal | Response |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AW (write address) | AWADDR, AWLEN, AWSIZE, AWBURST, AWID | master → slave | master | slave | none | none |
| W (write data) | WDATA, WSTRB, WLAST | master → slave | master | slave | WLAST | none |
| B (write response) | BRESP, BID | slave → master | slave | master | none | BRESP, one per transaction |
| AR (read address) | ARADDR, ARLEN, ARSIZE, ARBURST, ARID | master → slave | master | slave | none | none |
| R (read data) | RDATA, RRESP, RLAST, RID | slave → master | slave | master | RLAST | RRESP, one per beat |

Two details to keep. The write data and read data channels have a **LAST** signal that marks the final transfer of a burst (§A1.3.1). And there is **no read response channel**: the read response travels on R with the data, one RRESP per beat, while a write gets one BRESP for the whole burst (§A3.4.4). The four response values are OKAY (normal success), EXOKAY (an exclusive access succeeded), SLVERR (the slave reports an error) and DECERR (decode error: no slave at that address, usually returned by the interconnect) (§A3.4.4).

### The mail-delivery hook, and where it breaks

Now give each channel an everyday object. You (the master) are a customer; the store is the slave.

| Channel | In the analogy | What really moves |
|---|---|---|
| AW | the shipping label: where the box goes and how big it is | address and control, one per transaction |
| W | the box (a burst is several boxes, the last one marked) | one data beat per handshake; WLAST on the final beat |
| B | the delivery receipt | one write response per transaction |
| AR | the order form | address and control, one per transaction |
| R | the goods, each item with its own packing slip | one data beat with its RRESP per handshake; RLAST on the final beat |

**A write is two hand-overs in either order, then the receipt.** The label and the box go to separate counters. Either may be accepted first, or both on the same edge (IHI0022E §A3.3). Only the receipt has an order: in AXI4 the store mails it after it has accepted the label *and* the last box (§A3.3.1, AXI4 write response dependency). A read is an order form, then the goods; read data can never arrive before its order has been accepted (§A3.3, §A3.3.1).

**Where the mail hook breaks.**
- **A real label is stuck to its box.** An AXI label travels alone and may arrive before, with or after the box. Write data can even reach the slave first, for example when the AW path has more register stages (pipeline flip-flops) than the W path (§A3.3).
- **The customer never holds the box back waiting for the label counter.** A master must not wait for AWREADY or WREADY before asserting AWVALID or WVALID (§A3.3.1). The *store* may choose to wait: a slave can hold WREADY low until it sees AWVALID.
- **A real store could mail a receipt as soon as it reads the label.** An AXI4 slave must have both the AW handshake and the W handshake carrying WLAST first. Older AXI3 slaves could answer before taking the address; that is not AXI4-compliant (§A3.3.2).
- **One receipt per parcel, one slip per item.** B carries one response per write transaction; R carries a response with every beat, and the beats of one burst may report different responses (§A3.4.4).
- **Many orders can be open at once.** Orders with different IDs may come back in any order, and their beats may even be interleaved; orders with the same ID come back in order (§A5.3.1). No parcel service interleaves the items of two shipments.
- **No label number on the box.** In AXI4 nothing on W says which label it belongs to: boxes arrive in label order (WID was removed, §A5.4.1).

### VALID and READY: the courier and the door

All five channels use the same **handshake**: the agreement between source and destination about when one item has moved (IHI0022E §A3.2.1). Picture a **courier** (the source) holding out a parcel (the payload) at a **door** (the destination). VALID high means the courier is holding the parcel out; READY high means the door is open. The parcel changes hands only at a rising clock edge where both are true. That edge is the **transfer** (or handshake).

The complete rule set, all from IHI0022E §A3.2.1 unless marked:

1. **Transfer:** one transfer happens at each rising edge of ACLK (the AXI clock) where VALID and READY are both high.
2. **VALID never waits for READY:** a source must not wait until READY is asserted before asserting VALID.
3. **VALID holds:** once VALID is high, it stays high, with the payload stable, until the edge of the transfer. Per channel, §A3.2.2 says the same thing: for example, AWVALID must remain asserted until the rising clock edge after the slave asserts AWREADY.
4. **READY is free:** the destination may wait for VALID before raising READY, may raise READY before VALID arrives, and may lower READY again before VALID arrives.
5. **No combinational paths:** on master and slave interfaces no output may depend combinationally on an input.
6. **The only cross-channel dependencies:** RVALID only after the AR handshake; in AXI4, BVALID only after the AW handshake and the W handshake that carries WLAST (§A3.3.1). Apart from these, the protocol defines no relationship between channels (§A3.3).

**Where the courier breaks.** A real courier may wait for the door to open before holding out the parcel. An AXI source may not: rule 2. The door, however, is free to wait for the courier.

### Misconceptions to unlearn

1. **"Write data must follow the write address."** Wrong: the protocol defines no order between AW and W, and write data may appear first (IHI0022E §A3.3). A monitor or checker built on this belief raises false errors on legal traffic.
2. **"The three write steps are independent."** Wrong the other way: AW and W are independent of each other, but in AXI4 B depends on both (§A3.3.1). A slave model that sends BVALID after WLAST but before AWREADY is not AXI4-compliant.
3. **"READY must wait for VALID."** Wrong: the destination may raise READY first, and may lower it again before VALID arrives (§A3.2.1). A checker that flags early READY is wrong.
4. **"VALID may wait for READY."** Wrong, and dangerous: a source must not wait for READY (§A3.2.1). Two components that each wait for the other never transfer anything (the deadlock in the §A3.3.1 Caution).
5. **"A slow AHB slave stalls the whole system on chip (SoC)."** On one AHB-Lite bus a wait holds that bus's pipeline: the current data phase and the next address phase (IHI0033B.b §3.1). Whether other masters are affected depends on the interconnect, which the AHB specification does not define (§1.1.3).
6. **"An AXI interconnect is point to point inside."** AXI defines point-to-point *interfaces*; the interconnect behind them may share address or data buses (IHI0022E §A1.3.2).

### Where the hooks live in a testbench

Each hook turns into code you will write in the B-AHB and B-AXI lessons. A **driver** is the testbench component that turns transactions into pin activity; a **monitor** watches the pins and rebuilds transactions; a **checker** (often assertions) flags protocol violations; a **scoreboard** compares what the design did with what it should have done.

- **AXI driver (source side):** raise VALID with the payload as soon as the item is ready, without looking at READY, and hold both until the edge where READY is high. Never gate AWVALID on WREADY or WVALID on AWREADY.
- **AXI driver (destination side, for example a slave responder):** any READY pattern is legal, so randomize it. Back-pressure is how you find bugs in the design's VALID-hold logic.
- **AXI monitor:** record a transfer only at an edge where VALID and READY are both high. Accept W beats before their AW: queue them, and in AXI4 match them to write addresses in AW order.
- **AXI checker:** one VALID-hold assertion per channel, plus the cross-channel rules (B after AW and WLAST, R after AR). B-AXI-1 writes these.
- **AHB monitor:** take an address phase only at an edge where HREADY is high. A slave must sample HSELx, address and control only when HREADY is high (IHI0033B.b §4.2); otherwise a waited cycle is counted twice.

**When not to use the hooks.** Analogies help you predict and remember; they are never the specification. Write checks from the rules in the specification, with clause numbers in the assertion messages, and use the hooks only to explain a failure.

### Debugging with the hooks

| Symptom in the log or waveform | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Simulation hangs; one channel shows VALID low and READY low forever | VALID waits for READY while READY waits for VALID (the §A3.3.1 deadlock) | Make the source raise VALID independently of READY |
| Simulation hangs; AWVALID high, AWREADY low; the master never raises WVALID | The master waits for AWREADY before WVALID, and the slave waits for WVALID before AWREADY | Let the master offer W without waiting for AW (§A3.3.1 Caution) |
| Checker error "VALID dropped before READY", or a scoreboard missing item | The source withdrew or changed a pending item | Hold VALID and payload until the transfer edge (§A3.2.1) |
| False checker error on legal traffic: "W before AW" | Checker or monitor built on misconception 1 | Queue W beats until their AW arrives (§A3.3) |
| An AXI4 checker flags BVALID; the slave model passed AXI3 tests | B sent after WLAST but before the AW handshake | Hold B until both handshakes (§A3.3.1, §A3.3.2) |
| AHB monitor logs one address twice | Monitor sampled HADDR during a wait state | Sample address and control only when HREADY is high (IHI0033B.b §4.2) |

### Recap: the hooks on a waveform

- **HREADY low on AHB:** the truck at the bay is still unloading, and the next truck waits at the gate. Both the current data phase and the next address phase are held.
- **VALID high, READY low on AXI:** a courier holding the parcel still at a closed door. Legal for as long as it lasts, as long as the parcel does not change.
- **WVALID with no AWVALID:** a box at the counter with no label yet. Legal; the label may follow.
- **AWVALID with no WVALID:** a label with no box yet. Also legal.
- **BVALID:** the receipt. Before it may appear, find the AW handshake and the WLAST handshake.
- **RVALID:** goods with a slip. Before it may appear, find the AR handshake; then read RRESP on every beat.

### A handshake you can run

This bench models one VALID/READY channel, the handshake every AXI channel uses. The source sends three items. It raises VALID without looking at READY and holds VALID and the data until each transfer. The receiver's READY pattern is a fixed list that ignores VALID, which rule 4 allows. Both ends live in one process with nonblocking updates, so there is no design under test (DUT) and no race; B-AXI-1 drives a real design through a clocking block.

```systemverilog
// compile
// One VALID/READY channel (IHI0022E A3.2.1). The source never looks at READY
// before raising VALID; the receiver picks READY without looking at VALID.
module tb_top;
  timeunit 1ns;
  timeprecision 1ps;

  localparam logic [8:1] READY_AT = 8'b1110_1100; // READY at edges 3, 4, 6, 7, 8
  localparam int unsigned N_ITEMS  = 3;

  logic        clk   = 1'b0;
  logic        valid = 1'b1;   // item 0 is offered from time 0
  logic        ready = 1'b0;   // receiver's choice for edge 1
  logic [7:0]  data  = 8'hA0;
  int unsigned k     = 0;      // index of the current rising edge
  int unsigned sent  = 0;      // items transferred so far

  always #5 clk = ~clk;

  always @(posedge clk) begin
    k++;
    if (valid && ready) begin  // handshake at edge k
      $display("edge %0d: transfer of 8'h%h", k, data);
      sent++;
      if (sent < N_ITEMS) data  <= data + 8'h01;  // next item; VALID stays 1
      else                valid <= 1'b0;
    end
    // The receiver picks READY for edge k+1 without looking at VALID.
    ready <= (k < 8) ? READY_AT[k + 1] : 1'b1;
    if (k == 9) $finish;
  end

  // Hold rule: a stalled VALID stays high with the same data (A3.2.1).
  a_hold: assert property (@(posedge clk) valid && !ready |=> valid && $stable(data))
    else $error("VALID dropped or data changed before the transfer");
endmodule
```

Expected output (traced by hand from the code; no simulator run), followed by your simulator's `$finish` message:

```text
edge 3: transfer of 8'ha0
edge 4: transfer of 8'ha1
edge 6: transfer of 8'ha2
```

**Trace.** The `$display` reads `data` before the nonblocking update of the same edge, so each line prints the item that just moved.

| Edge | VALID | READY | DATA | Event |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 0 | A0 | stall: A0 held |
| 2 | 1 | 0 | A0 | stall: A0 held |
| 3 | 1 | 1 | A0 | transfer of A0; DATA becomes A1 |
| 4 | 1 | 1 | A1 | transfer of A1; DATA becomes A2 |
| 5 | 1 | 0 | A2 | stall: A2 held |
| 6 | 1 | 1 | A2 | transfer of A2; VALID falls |
| 7–8 | 0 | 1 | A2 | READY high, nothing to send: legal |
| 9 | 0 | 1 | A2 | `$finish` |

The assertion `a_hold` (a concurrent assertion, as taught in [I-SV-4A](/curriculum/T2_Intermediate/I-SV-4A_SVA_Fundamentals/index)) checks rule 3 at every stalled edge (1, 2 and 5) and passes each time.

**Break it on purpose.**

- **(a) Deadlock.** Make both ends wait for each other. Start with `valid = 1'b0`, replace the receiver line with `ready <= valid;`, and add a source that raises VALID only after seeing READY:

  ```systemverilog
  // snippet: changes to tb_top for break-it (a)
  logic valid = 1'b0;  // nothing offered yet
  // inside always @(posedge clk), replacing the READY line:
  // VALID waits for READY: illegal
  if (!valid && ready && sent < N_ITEMS) valid <= 1'b1;
  // READY waits for VALID: legal on its own
  ready <= valid;
  // after the always block:
  final if (sent == 0) $error("no transfer: VALID and READY waited for each other");
  ```

  Expected: no transfer lines, then that error when `$finish` ends the run. Each rule taken alone looks harmless; together they are the deadlock the §A3.3.1 Caution warns about, squeezed into one channel.

- **(b) Dropped VALID.** In the original bench, add `if (!ready) valid <= 1'b0;` to the end of the `always` block. Expected: `a_hold` fails at edge 2 (time 15 ns), because VALID was high with READY low at edge 1 and is low at edge 2. VALID never rises again, so no transfer lines appear.

**Run it.** Any simulator with SystemVerilog Assertions support compiles this single file with `tb_top` as the top. In Verilator, concurrent assertion support varies by version (tool-dependent), so check its documentation if `a_hold` is not reported.

### How to read any AMBA waveform with the hooks

1. **Find the transfers.** On each AXI channel, mark every rising edge where VALID and READY are both high. Nothing else moves data.
2. **Check the holds.** Between a VALID rise and its transfer, VALID must stay high and the payload must not change. The per-channel wording in IHI0022E §A3.2.2 says the same thing: VALID remains asserted until the rising edge after READY is asserted.
3. **Label the handshakes.** Name each one AW, W (and which carries WLAST), B, AR or R.
4. **Check the only dependencies.** Each B comes after its AW handshake and its WLAST handshake; each R beat comes after its AR handshake. Do not demand any other order.
5. **On AHB, find the HREADY-low edges.** At each one, both the current data phase and the next address phase are held. Read address and control only at edges where HREADY is high.

**Checklist before moving on:**
- [ ] I can name the VALID driver and the READY driver for each of the five channels.
- [ ] I can state the only cross-channel dependencies: R after AR; in AXI4, B after AW and WLAST.
- [ ] I can mark a described handshake legal or illegal and name the rule it follows or breaks.
- [ ] I can say what an AHB wait state holds, and why C's address in the pipeline diagram is accepted at edge 4.
- [ ] I can say where each of the four analogies breaks.

## Push Further

### Expert: where every analogy breaks

| Analogy | What it gets right | Where it breaks | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Two-stage bridge (AHB) | Address and data phases; a wait holds the next address; decoder and multiplexor | Two transfers are on the bridge at once; no arbiter in the protocol, so multi-master arbitration and multi-layer design live in the interconnect; each burst beat has its own address phase | IHI0033B.b §1.1, §1.1.3, §1.3, §2.2, §3.1, §3.2, §4.1 |
| Lanes into an interchange (AXI) | Separate one-way channels; many transactions in flight | The interconnect may share buses inside; channels have dependencies; same-ID ordering | IHI0022E §A1.3.2, §A3.3.1, §A5.3 |
| Mail delivery (channels) | AW, W, B, AR, R roles; no read response channel | W may lead AW; B needs AW and WLAST (AXI4); RRESP per beat; boxes in label order (AXI4); reads with different IDs interleave | IHI0022E §A3.3, §A3.3.1, §A3.4.4, §A5.3.1, §A5.4.1 |
| Courier and door (VALID/READY) | Transfer only when both are true; the parcel is held | The courier may not wait for the door; the door may open and close before the courier arrives | IHI0022E §A3.2.1 |

In an interview, giving the analogy *and* its limit is what separates a memorized picture from understanding.

### Expert: why VALID may never depend on READY

The rule exists so that any legal source can be connected to any legal destination, through any number of register slices and interconnect stages, without a deadlock. If VALID could wait for READY, a destination that legally waits for VALID would never raise READY: neither side moves (IHI0022E §A3.3.1, Caution). The asymmetry is deliberate: READY may depend on VALID, so exactly one side of every channel is allowed to wait.

The ban on combinational paths from inputs to outputs (§A3.2.1) closes the other loop: without it, a READY that depends combinationally on VALID, wired to a VALID that depends combinationally on READY through some other path, would form a combinational loop. AXI4-Stream uses the identical rule for TVALID and TREADY (IHI0051A §2.2.1), so a stream driver written from this lesson is already correct.

**READY defaults are a performance choice.** The specification recommends a default of HIGH for AWREADY and ARREADY, because a default of LOW forces every address transfer to take at least two cycles (§A3.2.2). WREADY, BREADY and RREADY may default HIGH only if that side can always accept in a single cycle (§A3.2.2).

### Expert: AXI3 legacy write responses

The AXI4 write response dependency (B after both AW and WLAST) was added because AXI3 did not state it. An AXI3 slave that accepts all write data and responds before accepting the address is not AXI4-compliant; reusing one in an AXI4 system needs a wrapper that holds the response until the address has been accepted (IHI0022E §A3.3.2). The specification's note adds why the rule matters: by issuing a write response, the slave takes responsibility for hazard-checking that write against later transactions (§A3.3.1). For verification, an AXI4 verification IP (VIP) must flag B-before-AW, and a mixed AXI3/AXI4 environment needs this check configurable per interface.

### Expert: reset and the first handshake

During reset (ARESETn low), a master must drive ARVALID, AWVALID and WVALID low, and a slave must drive RVALID and BVALID low; all other signals may take any value. The earliest a master may raise a VALID is at a rising ACLK edge after ARESETn is high (IHI0022E §A3.1.2). Two consequences for a testbench: VALID-hold assertions need a reset qualifier (for example `disable iff (!aresetn)`), and a driver must not leave VALID high through a mid-test reset. Reset handling across a whole environment is the subject of [A-UVM-9](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/A-UVM-9_Reset_and_Error_Injection/index).

### Expert: register slices and why the box can beat the label

Each AXI channel moves information in one direction only, and the architecture requires no fixed timing relationship between channels. That is why a **register slice** (a pipeline register on one channel) can be inserted at almost any point, at the cost of one cycle of latency per slice (IHI0022E §A1.3.3). It is also the physical reason W can arrive before AW: if the AW path has more register stages than the W path, the data simply gets there first (§A3.3). A monitor placed after a register slice can therefore see a different AW-to-W order than one placed at the master, and both must accept it.

### Expert: AHB, why only the data phase can stretch

A slave cannot extend the address phase, so every slave must be able to sample the address in one cycle (IHI0033B.b §1.3). It extends the data phase by driving HREADYOUT low, and the interconnect combines all HREADYOUT signals into the single HREADY every component sees (§3.1). Because HREADY is shared, the next transfer's address phase stretches too, and every slave must sample HSELx, address and control only when HREADY is high (§4.2). An AHB monitor or slave model that samples the address on every edge double-counts waited transfers. [B-AHB-1](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/B-AHB-1_AHB_Design_Timing_Mechanics/index) turns this into the normative timing rules.

| Channel | Payload direction | VALID driven by | READY driven by | Last-beat signal | Response carried |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AW | ? | ? | ? | ? | ? |
| W | ? | ? | ? | ? | ? |
| B | ? | ? | ? | ? | ? |
| AR | ? | ? | ? | ? | ? |
| R | ? | ? | ? | ? | ? |

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

| Channel | Payload direction | VALID driven by | READY driven by | Last-beat signal | Response carried |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AW | master → slave | master | slave | none | none |
| W | master → slave | master | slave | WLAST | none |
| B | slave → master | slave | master | none | BRESP, one per transaction |
| AR | master → slave | master | slave | none | none |
| R | slave → master | slave | master | RLAST | RRESP, one per beat |

The rule behind every row: VALID travels with the payload, READY against it (IHI0022E §A3.2.1).

### Drill 2: is this handshake legal?

Decide each case, and name the rule, before you open the answers. "Edge" means a rising clock edge.

1. VALID is high with D0 for three edges while READY is low; D0 does not change; the transfer happens at the first edge where READY is high.
2. A source raises VALID only after it has seen READY high.
3. READY pulses high for one cycle with no VALID; later VALID rises, and READY rises again two edges after that.
4. VALID is high with D0 at edge 1, READY is low, and at edge 2 VALID is low; no edge had READY high.
5. In an AXI4 write: WLAST handshake at edge 2, BVALID high at edge 3, AW handshake at edge 4.
6. RVALID is high at edge 1; the AR handshake for that read happens at edge 3.
7. In a write: AW handshake at edge 5 and the W handshake with WLAST at edge 5, BVALID first sampled high at edge 6.

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. **Legal.** VALID waits with a stable payload; that is the hold rule (IHI0022E §A3.2.1).
2. **Illegal.** A source must not wait for READY before asserting VALID (§A3.2.1).
3. **Legal.** READY may rise before VALID and fall again before VALID arrives (§A3.2.1).
4. **Illegal.** VALID was withdrawn before a handshake (§A3.2.1).
5. **Illegal in AXI4.** BVALID needs the AW handshake too (§A3.3.1). An AXI3 slave might do this, and it is not AXI4-compliant (§A3.3.2).
6. **Illegal.** RVALID only after the AR handshake (§A3.3.1).
7. **Legal.** AW and W may complete on the same edge (§A3.3), and BVALID follows both.

</details>
