## Quick Take

- **What it is:** AHB (the Advanced High-performance Bus, in its AHB-Lite and AHB5 versions) is a **pipelined** bus. Every transfer has an **address phase**, in which the master says what it wants, and a **data phase**, in which the data moves. The next transfer's address phase happens during the current transfer's data phase (Arm IHI0033B.b §1.3, §3.1).
- **Why it matters:** Every AHB driver, monitor and checker you write in [B-AHB-2](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/B-AHB-2_AHB_Pitfalls_and_Deadlocks/index) and [B-AHB-3](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/B-AHB-3_AHB_Verification/index) depends on one skill: knowing at which clock edge each phase starts and ends. Get it wrong by one edge and your monitor pairs data with the wrong address, or your checker flags legal traffic.
- **The Analogy:** A waiter takes the next table's order (the address phase) while the kitchen plates the current dish (the data phase). If the kitchen is slow (HREADY low), the waiter stands at the next table holding that order until the kitchen is ready. *Where it breaks:* a waiter with no order (IDLE) or on a pause (BUSY) may switch to a real order while waiting, and a held real order may be dropped only after the kitchen's first ERROR cycle. And on AHB the "kitchen" is one shared HREADY signal, so a slow slave makes everything on that bus wait, not just one table.

**You will be able to:**
1. Predict, cycle by cycle, the address and data phases of back-to-back transfers, and what HREADY low does to both.
2. Decode HTRANS and HBURST, and calculate every beat address of incrementing and wrapping bursts for a given HSIZE.
3. Explain when a slave samples HSELx, address and control, how HREADYOUT differs from HREADY, and why an incrementing burst must not cross a 1KB boundary.
4. Explain at timing level how an OKAY completion differs from the two-cycle ERROR response, and what the master may drive in the second ERROR cycle.

## Build Your Mental Model

**How to read this lesson.** The core path is five pictures: where AHB sits, the two phases, wait states, transfer types and bursts. Each is introduced with a question for you to answer before you look. Then come the 1KB rule, the ERROR response, two awareness topics (locked transfers and early burst termination), the lab, and how all of this shows up in a testbench. This page owns the normative AHB timing rules; [B-AHB-2](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/B-AHB-2_AHB_Pitfalls_and_Deadlocks/index) turns them into checks.

**Words you need first.**
- A **master** starts transfers (a CPU, a DMA engine); a **slave** answers them (a memory, a register block). IHI0033B.b uses these words; the later issue IHI0033C (2021) renames them **manager** and **subordinate** without changing the rules. This track says master and slave.
- A **transfer** is one read or write of one data item. A **burst** is a run of related transfers, and each transfer in a burst is a **beat**. (AHB says "transfer" where other protocols say "transaction".)
- **HCLK** is the bus clock. "All signal timings are related to the rising edge of HCLK" (Table 2-1), so "edge" on this page always means a rising edge of HCLK. A value is **sampled** at an edge when a flip-flop captures it there.
- A **phase** is a stretch of one or more clock cycles with one job. A **wait state** is a cycle in which a slave makes the transfer take longer.
- Every AHB signal name starts with H. A name ending in `x`, such as HSELx, means "one per slave": HSEL_S1, HSEL_S2 and so on (Table 2-4).
- **Address and control** means the address bus HADDR plus the signals that describe the transfer, such as its type (HTRANS), direction (HWRITE) and size (HSIZE). The full list is in the signal table below (Table 2-2).
- **HTRANS** says what each address phase is: **IDLE** (no transfer), **NONSEQ** (a new transfer, or the first beat of a burst), **SEQ** (a later beat of the same burst) or **BUSY** (the master pausing inside a burst). The full rules are under [Transfer types](#transfer-types-idle-busy-nonseq-seq).

### Where AHB sits: master, decoder, multiplexor

An AHB system with one master needs two pieces of glue logic, together called the **interconnect** (§1.1.3, §4.1):

- the **decoder** looks at the address and raises exactly one **HSELx** (select) signal, telling one slave "this transfer is for you" (§4.2);
- the **multiplexor** passes the selected slave's read data (HRDATA), response (HRESP) and ready (HREADYOUT) back to the master (§4.3).

The master drives the address, control and write data (HWDATA) to **every** slave at once; only the selected one acts on them. Each slave drives its own **HREADYOUT**; the multiplexor turns the selected slave's HREADYOUT into the single **HREADY** that the master and **all** slaves see (§3.1, Table 2-5). An address that no real slave owns goes to a **default slave**, which answers a real transfer with ERROR and an IDLE or BUSY with a zero-wait OKAY (§4.2.1).

Before you look: which signal tells a slave that it is *allowed* to look at the address bus, HSELx or HREADY? Hold your answer until the section on sampling.

**Why the select is retimed.** The decoder decodes the address during the address phase, but the multiplexor must route the slave that owns the *data* phase, one transfer later. So the multiplexor's control is registered into the data phase (§2.4 Note). With several masters, the interconnect also arbitrates and routes address, control and write data; how it does that, single-layer or multi-layer, is outside the specification (§1.1.3, §4.1).

### The signals, by phase

Learn the signals by *who drives them* and *in which phase they matter*. Address-phase signals belong to the transfer being offered; data-phase signals belong to the transfer that was accepted one step earlier.

| Signal | Driven by | Phase | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| HCLK | clock source | all | Times every transfer; everything is sampled at its rising edge (Table 2-1) |
| HRESETn | reset controller | all | The only active-low signal; resets the bus (Table 2-1, §7.1.2) |
| HADDR[31:0] | master | address | Byte address of the transfer (Table 2-2) |
| HTRANS[1:0] | master | address | Transfer type: IDLE, BUSY, NONSEQ or SEQ (§3.2) |
| HWRITE | master | address | 1 = write, 0 = read; constant through a burst (Table 2-2) |
| HSIZE[2:0] | master | address | Bytes per beat, 2^HSIZE; constant through a burst (§3.4) |
| HBURST[2:0] | master | address | Burst type: SINGLE, INCR, WRAP4/8/16, INCR4/8/16 (§3.5) |
| HPROT[3:0] | master | address | Protection: data or opcode, privileged, bufferable, modifiable (§3.7) |
| HMASTLOCK | master | address | This transfer is part of a locked sequence (§3.3) |
| HSELx | decoder | address | Selects one slave (Table 2-4, §4.2) |
| HWDATA | master | data | Write data, held until the data phase completes (§6.1.1) |
| HRDATA | slave, via multiplexor | data | Read data, valid only at the completing edge (§6.1.2) |
| HREADYOUT | each slave | data | 0 = "I need another cycle" (Table 2-3) |
| HREADY | multiplexor | data | 1 = the previous transfer is complete; seen by the master and all slaves (Table 2-5) |
| HRESP | slave, via multiplexor | data | 0 = OKAY, 1 = ERROR (§5.1, Table 5-1) |

The data buses are shown 32 bits wide; the protocol allows 8 to 1024 bits and recommends at least 32 (§6.3).

**AHB-Lite and AHB5 (awareness).** IHI0033B.b defines both versions; "AHB" means either (§1.2). AHB-Lite is the original issue A interface. AHB5 adds optional capabilities, each declared by a **property** that is False unless declared: Extended_Memory_Types (HPROT[6:4], §3.8), Secure_Transfers (HNONSEC, §3.9), Endian (§6.2), Stable_Between_Clock (§7.1.1), Exclusive_Transfers (HEXCL, HMASTER[3:0], HEXOKAY, Chapter 8) and Multi_Copy_Atomicity (§9.2), plus optional user signals HAUSER, HWUSER and HRUSER (§10.1). Every timing rule on this page applies to both versions. [Push Further](#expert-ahb5-properties-and-what-issue-c-added) lists what IHI0033C added.

### Address phase and data phase

The **address phase** lasts one cycle, unless the previous transfer extends it; the **data phase** "might require several HCLK cycles" (§3.1). A slave cannot stretch the address phase, so every slave must be able to sample the address in one cycle (§1.3).

**Predict first.** Number the columns below 0 to 4; column k ends at edge k, where every value in it is sampled. Transfer A's address phase is column 1, so A is accepted at edge 1. At which edge is A's write data sampled: edge 1 or edge 2?

**Debrief.** Edge 2. A's data phase is column 2, and the same edge 2 also accepts B's address, because B's address phase *is* column 2. Data B follows at edge 3. This overlap is the whole point of the pipeline: "the address phase of any transfer occurs during the data phase of the previous transfer" (§3.1). With no wait states the bus completes one transfer per cycle, even though each transfer takes two.

**Misconception: "a transfer's address and data happen in the same cycle."** They never do. A transfer's data is always at least one edge after its address was accepted. If you read the waveform by looking down a single column, you pair B's address with A's data. Read it diagonally instead: the address **accepted** at edge k (HREADY high at edge k) belongs with the data at the next edge after k that has HREADY high.

### Wait states: HREADY low holds both phases

A slave that needs more time drives its HREADYOUT low; the interconnect turns that into HREADY low. Each cycle that ends with HREADY low while HRESP is OKAY is a **wait state**, and the data phase is extended by one cycle (§3.1, §5.1.2). The only other cycle that ends with HREADY low is the first cycle of an ERROR response, covered under [Responses](#responses-okay-wait-states-and-the-two-cycle-error).

**Predict first.** The slave inserts one wait state in A's data phase. B's address is already on the bus in that cycle. Does B's address get accepted on time (one edge after A's), or does it wait too?

**Debrief.** B waits too. At edge 2 HREADY is low, so nothing is sampled: not A's data and not B's address. Both are held, and both are taken at edge 3. The specification says it directly: extending a data phase "has the side-effect of extending the address phase of the next transfer" (§3.1, Figure 3-5).

**Misconception: "a wait state stalls only the data phase."** It stalls the next address phase as well, because both are released by the same HREADY. A driver that moves on to its next address while HREADY is low has just dropped a transfer.

The rules that follow from this:

- **When data moves.** Write data is sampled, or read data captured, only at an edge where HREADY is high. The master holds HWDATA stable for the whole data phase (§6.1.1). A slave only has to drive valid HRDATA in the final cycle (§6.1.2).
- **HREADYOUT is not HREADY.** HREADYOUT is one slave's output. HREADY is the multiplexed result that everyone obeys. A slave that watches only its own HREADYOUT does not know when *another* slave's transfer has finished.
- **Wait states are bounded by design.** "In general, every slave must have a predetermined maximum number of wait states" so that worst-case latency can be calculated (§5.1.2 Note). IHI0033B.b also recommends no more than 16, except for devices such as a serial boot ROM; IHI0033C keeps the predetermined maximum but drops the number. Checking a bound is integration policy, not a protocol rule, as B-AHB-2 explains.
- **The master holds what is waiting.** Once a NONSEQ or SEQ is on the bus during a wait, the master keeps HTRANS and the address until HREADY is high. The exceptions involve IDLE, BUSY and the ERROR response, and are listed in [Push Further](#expert-the-complete-waited-transfer-rules).

### When a slave samples: HSELx and HREADY

Back to the question from the first section. HSELx says *which* slave the address phase is for. HREADY says *whether the bus is moving*. A slave "must only sample the HSELx, address, and control signals when HREADY is HIGH, indicating that the current transfer is completing" (§4.2). The decoder is combinational, so HSELx follows HADDR immediately, including while HREADY is low; "it is possible that HSELx is asserted when HREADY is LOW, but the selected slave has changed by the time the current transfer completes" (§4.2).

Notice also that HSEL_S2 was high at edge 2 for an IDLE that S2 never acts on: the master may change an IDLE's address and type while it waits (§3.6.1, §3.6.2).

### Transfer types: IDLE, BUSY, NONSEQ, SEQ

HTRANS tells every slave what kind of address phase is on the bus (§3.2, Table 3-1):

| HTRANS | Type | Meaning | What the slave does |
|---|---|---|---|
| `2'b00` | IDLE | No data transfer wanted | Zero-wait OKAY, and ignore it |
| `2'b01` | BUSY | Pause in the middle of a burst; address and control already show the *next* beat | Zero-wait OKAY, and ignore it |
| `2'b10` | NONSEQ | A single transfer, or the first beat of a burst; unrelated to the previous transfer | Perform it |
| `2'b11` | SEQ | A later beat of a burst: HWRITE, HSIZE, HBURST and HPROT as in the previous beat; address = previous + size (or wrapped) | Perform it |

Notice that HTRANS[1] is 1 exactly for the two real transfer types, NONSEQ and SEQ. Monitors use that bit.

**BUSY versus a wait state.** Both make a burst slower, but different sides cause them. BUSY is the *master* saying "I am not ready for the next beat yet"; a wait state is the *slave* saying "I am not done with this beat" (HREADYOUT low). A master cannot insert wait states, and a slave cannot insert BUSY.

**Predict first.** Below, an INCR4 read of words starts at 0x20 and the master inserts one BUSY before the second beat. At which edge does the master capture the read data for 0x24?

**Debrief.** Edge 4. The BUSY delays the SEQ to 0x24 by one cycle, so 0x24 is accepted at edge 3 and its data comes at edge 4. The BUSY's own "data phase" at edge 3 carries nothing. The rules for BUSY (§3.2, §3.5.1):

- BUSY appears only *between* beats of a burst, never after a SINGLE. A SINGLE must be followed by IDLE or NONSEQ.
- A fixed-length burst (INCR4/8/16, WRAP4/8/16) must end with a SEQ, not a BUSY.
- An undefined-length INCR *may* end with BUSY, followed by NONSEQ or IDLE; that ends the burst.

### Bursts: HBURST, HSIZE and beat addresses

**HSIZE** gives the bytes in each beat, 2^HSIZE (§3.4, Table 3-2). It must not exceed the data bus width and stays constant for the whole burst.

| HSIZE | Bits | Name |
|---|---|---|
| `3'b000` | 8 | byte |
| `3'b001` | 16 | halfword |
| `3'b010` | 32 | word |
| `3'b011` | 64 | doubleword |
| `3'b100` | 128 | 4-word line |
| `3'b101` | 256 | 8-word line |
| `3'b110` | 512 | |
| `3'b111` | 1024 | |

**HBURST** gives the number of beats and the address pattern (§3.5, Table 3-3). The number is beats, not bytes: an INCR4 of words moves 16 bytes.

| HBURST | Type | Beats |
|---|---|---|
| `3'b000` | SINGLE | 1 |
| `3'b001` | INCR | undefined |
| `3'b010` | WRAP4 | 4 |
| `3'b011` | INCR4 | 4 |
| `3'b100` | WRAP8 | 8 |
| `3'b101` | INCR8 | 8 |
| `3'b110` | WRAP16 | 16 |
| `3'b111` | INCR16 | 16 |

A single transfer can be sent two ways: a SINGLE burst, or an undefined-length INCR of one beat (§3.5). Coverage models and decoders must accept both.

**The two address rules.** Let `size = 2^HSIZE` bytes.
- **Incrementing** (INCR, INCR4/8/16): `next = addr + size`. The address keeps climbing.
- **Wrapping** (WRAP4/8/16): the burst stays inside a block of `block = beats × size` bytes that is aligned to its own size. "The address boundary is calculated as the product of the number of beats in a burst and the size of the transfer" (§3.5). So `next = (addr & ~(block - 1)) | ((addr + size) & (block - 1))`: keep the block's upper bits, let the lower bits roll over.

A wrapping burst does not have to start at the start of its block; that is what makes it useful for cache-line fills, which fetch the word the processor asked for first. **Alignment** is separate: every beat must be aligned to its own size, for example `HADDR[1:0] = 2'b00` for words (§3.5). IHI0033B.b also requires the address of an IDLE transfer to be aligned, so that bus monitors do not report spurious warnings (§3.5); IHI0033C turns this into a recommendation (IHI0033C §3.6).

**Traced example: WRAP8 of words from 0x34.** Here `size = 4` and `block = 8 × 4 = 32 = 0x20`, so the block is 0x20 to 0x3F.

| Beat | addr | addr + 4 | low bits `& 0x1F` | block base `0x20` OR low bits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0x34 | | | **0x34** (start) |
| 1 | 0x34 | 0x38 | 0x18 | **0x38** |
| 2 | 0x38 | 0x3C | 0x1C | **0x3C** |
| 3 | 0x3C | 0x40 | 0x00 | **0x20** (wrapped) |
| 4 | 0x20 | 0x24 | 0x04 | **0x24** |
| 5 | 0x24 | 0x28 | 0x08 | **0x28** |
| 6 | 0x28 | 0x2C | 0x0C | **0x2C** |
| 7 | 0x2C | 0x30 | 0x10 | **0x30** |

This matches the specification's Figure 3-10: 0x34, 0x38, 0x3C, 0x20, 0x24, 0x28, 0x2C, 0x30. A WRAP4 of words from 0x34 uses a 16-byte block instead and gives 0x34, 0x38, 0x3C, 0x30 (§3.5). An INCR8 of halfwords from 0x34 never wraps: 0x34, 0x36, …, 0x40, 0x42 (Figure 3-11).

**Misconception: "WRAP8 of words from 0x34 wraps at 0x30."** That is the WRAP4 block size. The block grows with the beat count: 16 bytes for WRAP4 of words, 32 bytes for WRAP8.

### The 1KB rule

"Masters must not attempt to start an incrementing burst that crosses a 1KB address boundary" (§3.5). The reason is in the address map: "the minimum address space that can be allocated to a single slave is 1KB", and every slave region starts and ends on a 1KB boundary (§4.2). If incrementing bursts never cross 1KB, no burst ever crosses from one slave into another.

**Debrief.** S1 sees a burst that stops after two beats, and S2 sees a burst that starts with a SEQ. Neither slave can be expected to handle that, which is why the rule binds the *master*. What the rule covers:

- **Every incrementing burst**, including undefined-length INCR. An INCR master must stop before the boundary and start a new burst with NONSEQ at the 1KB address.
- **Not wrapping bursts.** A WRAP burst never leaves its own `beats × size` block, so it needs no 1KB rule; it can never wander into the next region. (One exotic exception is in [Push Further](#expert-can-a-legal-burst-span-two-slave-regions).)

**Misconception: "AHB INCR bursts have no boundary rule."** They do: 1KB. The 4KB rule you may know belongs to AXI (IHI0022E §A3.4.1, taught in [B-AXI-2](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/B-AXI-2_AXI_Burst_Math/index)). Since a legal AHB incrementing burst never crosses 1KB, it never crosses 4KB either, which is why an AHB-to-AXI bridge never splits a burst for 4KB reasons ([B-AMBA-F1](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/B-AMBA-F1_Bridges_and_System_Integration/index)).

### Responses: OKAY, wait states and the two-cycle ERROR

The slave reports the outcome on HRESP, and the *combination* of HRESP and HREADYOUT tells the master what is happening (§5.1, Table 5-2):

| | HRESP = 0 (OKAY) | HRESP = 1 (ERROR) |
|---|---|---|
| **HREADYOUT = 0** | transfer pending (wait state) | ERROR response, first cycle |
| **HREADYOUT = 1** | transfer completed successfully | ERROR response, second cycle |

**OKAY** can finish in one cycle (§5.1.1). **ERROR** always takes two: first HRESP high with HREADYOUT low, then HRESP high with HREADYOUT high (§5.1.3). Why two? "By the time a slave starts to issue an ERROR response then the address for the following transfer has already been broadcast onto the bus." The extra cycle gives the master time to see the error and cancel that next transfer by driving IDLE (§5.1.3). If the slave needs longer to decide, it inserts ordinary OKAY wait states *before* the two ERROR cycles (§5.1.3).

After an ERROR, the master chooses (§3.5.2):
- **cancel**: drive HTRANS to IDLE in the second ERROR cycle, so the access already on the address bus is never accepted; it does not have to rebuild the burst later;
- **continue**: keep the next beat on the bus unchanged; it is accepted at the second ERROR cycle's edge, where HREADY is high.

- **ERROR read data is not valid.** "A slave only has to provide valid data when a transfer completes with an OKAY response. ERROR responses do not require valid read data" (§6.1.2). A monitor must not compare HRDATA from an ERROR completion.
- **A one-cycle ERROR is a bug.** HRESP high with HREADY high, without the low-HREADY cycle first, breaks §5.1.3. [B-AHB-2](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/B-AHB-2_AHB_Pitfalls_and_Deadlocks/index) shows the assertion that catches it.

- an IDLE after a locked transfer is recommended;
- all transfers in a locked sequence must go to the same slave address region (a requirement added after issue A, so verify legacy masters for it);

### Ending a burst early (awareness)

A master is not permitted to end a burst early on its own (§3.5.2). The specification names two ways a burst can still be terminated early (§3.5.2):
- **after an ERROR**, if the master chooses to cancel (above);
- **by a multi-layer interconnect**, which may end a burst so that another master can reach the slave. Slaves "must be designed to work correctly if the burst is not completed" (§3.5.2).

An **undefined-length INCR** is not terminated early, because it has no fixed last beat: it simply ends whenever the master sends NONSEQ or IDLE, possibly after a BUSY (§3.5.1).

So a slave that prefetches the rest of a burst, or counts beats and expects the last one, must tolerate the burst stopping. A checker must not demand that every fixed-length burst completes.

### In your testbench

Everything above becomes one rule for a **monitor** (the testbench component that watches the bus and reports each completed transfer): **an address phase is accepted at an edge with HREADY high and HTRANS[1] = 1 (plus HSELx for a slave-side monitor); its data and response are taken at the next edge with HREADY high.** A **driver** (the component that drives the master's signals) mirrors it: it keeps each NONSEQ or SEQ on the bus until an edge with HREADY high, then presents the next one.

```systemverilog
// snippet: the sampling core of an AHB monitor's run_phase (full monitor in B-AHB-3).
// vif.mon_cb is a clocking block that samples the bus at posedge HCLK.
forever begin
  @(vif.mon_cb);
  if (!vif.mon_cb.HREADY) continue;               // HREADY low: nothing starts or ends
  if (pend_valid) begin                           // 1. the pending data phase ends here
    pend.err = vif.mon_cb.HRESP;                  // 1 here is the ERROR second cycle
    if (pend.write) pend.data = vif.mon_cb.HWDATA;
    else if (!pend.err) pend.data = vif.mon_cb.HRDATA;  // ERROR: no valid read data
    ap.write(pend);
    pend_valid = 0;
  end
  // 2. NONSEQ or SEQ accepted here. HSEL makes this a slave-side monitor;
  //    a master-side monitor drops the HSEL term.
  if (vif.mon_cb.HSEL && vif.mon_cb.HTRANS[1]) begin
    pend = ahb_item::type_id::create("pend");
    pend.addr  = vif.mon_cb.HADDR;
    pend.write = vif.mon_cb.HWRITE;
    pend_valid = 1;
  end
end
```

The order inside the loop matters: at one edge the previous transfer's data phase ends *and* the next address phase is accepted, so the monitor closes the old transfer before it opens the new one.

**Debugging: symptom → cause → fix.**

| Symptom in the log or scoreboard | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Every waited transfer is reported twice | The monitor samples the address at every edge, ignoring HREADY | Accept an address phase only at an edge with HREADY high (§4.2) |
| Data is paired with the wrong address, shifted by one transfer | Address and data taken from the same edge | Pair each accepted address with the data at the *next* HREADY-high edge (§3.1) |
| Read data mismatches only on slow slaves | HRDATA captured in a wait cycle | Capture only at the completing edge (§6.1.2) |
| Extra transfers appear during bursts | BUSY counted as a transfer | Use HTRANS[1]: only NONSEQ and SEQ are transfers (§3.2) |
| Scoreboard mismatch right after an ERROR | HRDATA compared on an ERROR completion | Skip read data when HRESP is ERROR (§6.1.2) |
| Driver loses a transfer when the slave waits | The driver advanced its address while HREADY was low | Hold address and control until an HREADY-high edge (§3.1) |
| SEQ address mismatch at the wrap point of WRAP bursts | The driver or reference model adds the size for WRAP bursts too | Use the wrap formula, `next_addr()` in Make It Work (§3.5) |
| The 1KB checker fires on a legal WRAP4 at 0x3F8 | The checker applies the 1KB rule to wrapping bursts | Apply it to incrementing bursts only (§3.5) |
| A checker reports an incomplete INCR8 after an ERROR | The master cancelled the rest of the burst, which is legal | Allow early termination after ERROR and by a multi-layer interconnect (§3.5.2) |

Checkers that encode these rules, and the naive versions that flag legal traffic, are the subject of [B-AHB-2](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/B-AHB-2_AHB_Pitfalls_and_Deadlocks/index). The complete agent is built in [B-AHB-3](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/B-AHB-3_AHB_Verification/index).

### Recap: reading AHB on a waveform

| Question | Where to look |
|---|---|
| Is the bus moving at this edge? | HREADY = 1 |
| Which transfer is accepted at this edge? | The NONSEQ or SEQ on HADDR/HTRANS in the column ending at an HREADY-high edge |
| When is its data taken? | The next edge with HREADY = 1 |
| Is this a real transfer? | HTRANS[1] = 1 (NONSEQ or SEQ); IDLE and BUSY get a zero-wait OKAY |
| What is the next burst address? | INCR: add 2^HSIZE. WRAP: add, then wrap inside the `beats × size` block |
| Is this incrementing burst legal? | No beat may leave the first beat's 1KB region |
| Did it fail? | HRESP = 1 for two cycles: HREADY 0, then HREADY 1 |

## Make It Work

### A burst-address calculator you can run

This package encodes Tables 3-2 and 3-3 and the two address rules, and `tb_top` prints the beat addresses for six bursts, flagging any incrementing burst that crosses 1KB. The same `next_addr()` logic is what an AHB driver uses to generate SEQ addresses and what a monitor or checker uses to predict them.

```systemverilog
// compile
// File ahb_burst_math.sv: AHB burst addresses and the 1KB rule.
// IHI0033B.b Table 3-2 (HSIZE), Table 3-3 (HBURST), section 3.5 (rules).
package ahb_burst_pkg;

  // HBURST[2:0] encodings, Table 3-3.
  typedef enum logic [2:0] {
    SINGLE = 3'b000,
    INCR   = 3'b001,
    WRAP4  = 3'b010,
    INCR4  = 3'b011,
    WRAP8  = 3'b100,
    INCR8  = 3'b101,
    WRAP16 = 3'b110,
    INCR16 = 3'b111
  } hburst_e;

  // HSIZE[2:0] means 2**HSIZE bytes per beat, Table 3-2.
  function automatic int unsigned size_bytes(logic [2:0] hsize);
    return 1 << hsize;
  endfunction

  // Beats in a fixed-length burst. INCR has no fixed length (returns 0),
  // so a caller that models INCR supplies its own beat count.
  function automatic int unsigned fixed_beats(hburst_e b);
    case (b)
      SINGLE:         return 1;
      WRAP4, INCR4:   return 4;
      WRAP8, INCR8:   return 8;
      WRAP16, INCR16: return 16;
      default:        return 0;
    endcase
  endfunction

  function automatic bit is_wrap(hburst_e b);
    return b inside {WRAP4, WRAP8, WRAP16};
  endfunction

  // Every beat is aligned to its own size (section 3.5).
  function automatic bit is_aligned(logic [31:0] addr, logic [2:0] hsize);
    return (addr & (size_bytes(hsize) - 1)) == 0;
  endfunction

  // Address of the next beat. Incrementing bursts add the size; wrapping
  // bursts stay inside their (beats x size)-byte block (section 3.5).
  function automatic logic [31:0] next_addr(logic [31:0] addr, hburst_e b,
                                            logic [2:0] hsize);
    logic [31:0] size;
    logic [31:0] block;
    size = size_bytes(hsize);
    if (!is_wrap(b)) return addr + size;
    block = fixed_beats(b) * size;
    return (addr & ~(block - 1)) | ((addr + size) & (block - 1));
  endfunction

endpackage

module tb_top;
  import ahb_burst_pkg::*;

  // Prints every beat address. For an incrementing burst it also reports the
  // first beat that leaves the start beat's 1KB region (sections 3.5, 4.2).
  task automatic show_burst(hburst_e b, logic [2:0] hsize, logic [31:0] start,
                            int unsigned n_beats);
    logic [31:0] addr;
    string       line;
    string       verdict;
    addr    = start;
    verdict = "";
    line    = $sformatf("%s size=%0d start=0x%3h:", b.name(), size_bytes(hsize), start);
    if (!is_aligned(start, hsize)) verdict = " -> ILLEGAL: start not aligned to size";
    for (int unsigned i = 0; i < n_beats; i++) begin
      line = {line, $sformatf(" %3h", addr)};
      // The 1KB region is addr[31:10]; WRAP bursts never leave their block.
      if (!is_wrap(b) && verdict == "" && addr[31:10] != start[31:10])
        verdict = $sformatf(" -> ILLEGAL: crosses 1KB at 0x%3h", addr);
      addr = next_addr(addr, b, hsize);
    end
    $display("%s%s", line, verdict);
  endtask

  initial begin
    show_burst(WRAP4, 3'b010, 32'h034, fixed_beats(WRAP4));  // section 3.5 text
    show_burst(WRAP8, 3'b010, 32'h034, fixed_beats(WRAP8));  // Figure 3-10
    show_burst(INCR8, 3'b001, 32'h034, fixed_beats(INCR8));  // Figure 3-11
    show_burst(WRAP4, 3'b010, 32'h3F8, fixed_beats(WRAP4));
    show_burst(INCR4, 3'b010, 32'h3F8, fixed_beats(INCR4));
    show_burst(INCR,  3'b010, 32'h3F8, 3);                   // undefined length
    $finish;
  end
endmodule
```

**Run it.** Save the block as `ahb_burst_math.sv`, compile it with any IEEE 1800-2023 simulator, and run top module `tb_top`. Open-source support for this file was not verified for this page.

**How to read any AHB waveform, in six steps.**
1. Mark every edge where HREADY is 1. Nothing is accepted or completed at any other edge.
2. At each marked edge, look at HTRANS in the column that ends there. NONSEQ or SEQ means that transfer's address phase is accepted now; IDLE and BUSY mean nothing is.
3. For each accepted transfer, find the *next* marked edge. Its data (HWDATA or HRDATA) and HRESP are taken there.
4. Between those two edges, every edge with HREADY 0 and HRESP OKAY is a wait state, and the following address phase is held through all of them. (HRESP 1 with HREADY 0 is the first ERROR cycle; see step 6.)
5. For a burst, check each SEQ address against `next_addr()` and check that no incrementing beat leaves the first beat's 1KB region.
6. If HRESP is 1 with HREADY 0, the next edge must also have HRESP 1, with HREADY 1. Then see whether the master cancelled (IDLE in that second cycle) or continued.

**Checklist before moving on:**
- [ ] I can say at which edge a transfer's data is taken, given the edge that accepted its address and the HREADY trace.
- [ ] I can explain why a wait state also delays the next transfer's address phase.
- [ ] I know that a slave samples HSELx, address and control only at an edge with HREADY high.
- [ ] I can decode HTRANS and HBURST, and compute WRAP and INCR beat addresses for any HSIZE.
- [ ] I can tell whether an incrementing burst crosses 1KB, and explain why WRAP bursts are exempt.
- [ ] I can draw the two-cycle ERROR response and say what a cancelling master drives in the second cycle.

## Push Further

### Expert: the complete waited-transfer rules

"The master holds what is waiting" is the working rule. The full rule set is in §3.6.1 (transfer type) and §3.6.2 (address), Figures 3-13 to 3-17:

- **IDLE → NONSEQ.** During a wait, an IDLE may change to NONSEQ, and the address of an IDLE may change. Once HTRANS is NONSEQ, "the master must keep HTRANS constant, until HREADY is HIGH" (§3.6.1) and "must keep the address constant" (§3.6.2).
- **BUSY → SEQ, fixed-length burst.** A waited BUSY may become SEQ (only between beats, so never after a SINGLE); then HTRANS is held until HREADY is high (§3.6.1).
- **BUSY → anything, undefined-length INCR.** A waited BUSY may change to any type. SEQ continues the burst; IDLE or NONSEQ ends it (§3.6.1).
- **After an ERROR.** Once the first ERROR cycle has ended with HREADY low, the master may drive IDLE and change the address; the new values are on the bus in the second ERROR cycle (§3.6.2 "After an ERROR response", Figure 3-17, §3.5.2).

§3.6.2 opens with "the master can only change the address once, except as described" in the IDLE and ERROR cases. Read literally, that might seem to allow one free address change of any waited transfer. The standard checker reading, used by [B-AHB-2](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/B-AHB-2_AHB_Pitfalls_and_Deadlocks/index), B-AHB-3 and the lab model, is stricter, and it is derived from three places: the transfer type must not change during a wait (§3.6.1); after IDLE becomes NONSEQ the address is held (§3.6.2); and Figure 3-16 says "Until HREADY goes HIGH, no more address changes are permitted". So a waited NONSEQ or SEQ is held, and the one change §3.6.2 counts is the IDLE-to-NONSEQ step.

### Expert: HSELx timing and the retimed multiplexor select

IHI0033B.b calls HSELx "a combinatorial decode of the address bus" (Table 2-4), so it moves whenever HADDR moves, including during waits; that is why §4.2 forbids sampling it without HREADY. IHI0033C's Table 2-4 states the requirement directly: for a non-IDLE transfer "HSELx must be asserted in the same cycle as the address", while for IDLE it "can be asserted or deasserted". The multiplexor needs the *data-phase* owner, so its select must be "retimed to the data phase" (§2.4 Note). A multiplexor driven straight from the combinational HSELx routes the next slave's HREADYOUT, HRESP and HRDATA one transfer too early: the bus then waits on, or errors from, the wrong slave. One slave interface may also have several HSELx inputs, each a separate decode of at least 1KB, for example registers and a data window in different places in the map (§4.2.2).

### Expert: can a legal burst span two slave regions?

The guarantee in §4.2 is that incrementing transfers never cross 1KB, so "a burst never crosses an address decode boundary". For wrapping bursts the arithmetic is different. A WRAP16 of 1024-bit beats (HSIZE = `3'b111`, 128 bytes) has a block of 16 × 128 = 2048 bytes, which covers two 1KB regions. Such a burst is legal by §3.5 and can visit addresses in both halves of its 2KB block. IHI0033B.b does not discuss this case; it is arithmetic from Table 3-2 and §3.5, so treat it as a design-review question: on a 1024-bit bus, either both 1KB halves of every 2KB block belong to one slave, or the masters must never issue WRAP16 of 1024-bit beats. A WRAP block of 1KB or less never spans two regions, because both are aligned powers of two.

### Expert: bus width, narrow transfers and byte lanes

HSIZE must not exceed the bus width (§3.4 Note): on a 32-bit bus, only byte, halfword and word. Widths of 8 to 1024 bits are allowed and at least 32 is recommended (§6.3). A transfer narrower than the bus uses only its byte lanes: the master drives only those lanes of HWDATA and the slave reads only those lanes of HRDATA (§6.1.1, §6.1.2); which lanes depends on endianness (§6.2, BE8 and BE32). A slave adapted to a wider bus may answer a transfer wider than its natural interface with ERROR (§6.3). IHI0033C adds optional write strobes, HWSTRB, one bit per byte lane, which allow sparse writes (IHI0033C §3.5).

### Expert: AHB5 properties and what Issue C added

AHB5 capabilities are declared as properties, and a property that is not declared is False (IHI0033B.b §1.2). When you connect two components, check each property pair: some mismatches are harmless and some are not. IHI0033C gives interoperability tables for its new properties. For example, a manager with write strobes can drive a subordinate without them only if it never makes sparse writes, that is, writes that leave some byte lanes of a beat unwritten (IHI0033C §3.5.3, Table 3-3). Stable_Between_Clock is worth knowing for checkers: "stable" in AHB means the same value *at* successive rising edges in an extended transfer, and a signal may glitch between edges unless this property is True (§7.1.1). An SVA `$stable` check therefore matches the protocol meaning; glitch freedom between edges is not something a cycle-based checker can see.

IHI0033C (2021) keeps every rule taught on this page and adds, among other things (IHI0033C §1.2, Appendix B Table B-3):
- signal width properties, such as ADDR_WIDTH (HADDR was fixed at 32 bits in issues A and B);
- write strobes (§3.5) and interface parity protection (Chapter 12);
- a signal validity chapter: HTRANS, HADDR, HSEL, HMASTLOCK, HREADY, HREADYOUT and HRESP must always be valid, while HBURST, HPROT, HSIZE and HWRITE must be valid only when HTRANS is not IDLE (§8.2);
- the recommendation to drive HRDATA to zero on an ERROR read response (§5.1.3);
- IDLE address alignment downgraded from a rule to a recommendation (§3.6; it was a rule in IHI0033B.b §3.5);
- no "16 wait states" recommendation any more: only the predetermined maximum remains (§5.1.2 Note);
- a fourth user signal, HBUSER, for the response (§11.1).

Because a section was inserted, IHI0033C renumbers chapter 3 from §3.5 onwards; the map is in References.

### Expert: single-layer and multi-layer interconnects

IHI0033B.b defines one master interface. Systems with several masters need an interconnect that arbitrates and routes, and "further details of the different approaches used for multi-master systems, such as single layer or multi-layer interconnects, are not provided within this specification" (§4.1). On a single-layer bus, one master owns the bus at a time, and one slow slave stalls everyone through the shared HREADY. A multi-layer interconnect can let masters reach different slaves in parallel, and may terminate a burst so another master can reach a slave (§3.5.2). How it does this is product-specific: **tool-dependent** (interconnect IP), and documented by Arm in DVI 0045, which was not checked for this page. For a throughput comparison with AXI's independent channels, see [B-AXI-1](/curriculum/T3_Advanced/B-AXI-1_AXI_Channel_Architecture/index).

### Kata: burst addresses by hand

Work these on paper, then open the answers. Use `block = beats × size` and the wrap formula from Build Your Mental Model.

1. List all eight addresses of a WRAP8 burst of words (HSIZE = `3'b010`) starting at 0x34.
2. List all eight addresses of an INCR8 burst of halfwords (HSIZE = `3'b001`) starting at 0x34.
3. Is an INCR4 burst of words starting at 0x3F8 legal? If not, at which beat does it fail, and how should the master send the same data?
4. A WRAP4 burst of words starts at 0x3F8. List its addresses. Is it legal?

<details>
<summary>Worked answers</summary>

1. size = 4, block = 32 (0x20 to 0x3F): **0x34, 0x38, 0x3C, 0x20, 0x24, 0x28, 0x2C, 0x30**. From 0x3C, `(0x3C + 4) & 0x1F = 0x00`, so the block base 0x20 comes next (IHI0033B.b §3.5, Figure 3-10).
2. size = 2, incrementing, no wrap: **0x34, 0x36, 0x38, 0x3A, 0x3C, 0x3E, 0x40, 0x42** (Figure 3-11). It crosses a 16-byte boundary, which is fine; only 1KB matters.
3. **Illegal.** The beats would be 0x3F8, 0x3FC, 0x400, 0x404; the third beat crosses into the next 1KB region (§3.5). The master can send an undefined-length INCR (or two SINGLEs) for 0x3F8 and 0x3FC, then start a new burst with NONSEQ at 0x400.
4. block = 16 (0x3F0 to 0x3FF): **0x3F8, 0x3FC, 0x3F0, 0x3F4**. **Legal**: a WRAP burst stays in its block, and the 1KB rule applies to incrementing bursts only.

Check your answers against the Make It Work output, or run the lab's **WRAP vs INCR** scenario with start address 0x3F8.
