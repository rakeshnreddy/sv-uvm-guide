# AHB Protocol Design & Timing

In the previous module, we established that AHB is fundamentally a **shared bus**. Because only one master can use the bus at a time, the protocol must be highly optimized to maximize throughput. 

AHB achieves this optimization through **pipelining**. It overlaps the address phase of one transaction with the data phase of the previous transaction.

<QuickTake>
- **Pipelining:** The Address Phase of Transaction N happens at the same time as the Data Phase of Transaction N-1.
- **HREADY / HREADYOUT:** The selected slave drives `HREADYOUT` low to insert wait states. The interconnect multiplexes this into the global `HREADY` signal, extending the data phase (which consequently stalls the address phase of the next transaction).
- **HTRANS:** Defines the type of transfer (IDLE, BUSY, NONSEQ, SEQ).
</QuickTake>

---

## 1. Signal Taxonomy

Before looking at timing, let's categorize the core AHB signals:

### Master $\rightarrow$ Slave (Address & Control)
These signals are driven by the Master during the **Address Phase**:
- `HADDR [31:0]`: The byte address of the transfer.
- `HWRITE`: High for Write, Low for Read.
- `HTRANS [1:0]`: Transfer type (IHI0033B.b §3.2, Table 3-1).
  - `00` (IDLE): The master wants no transfer. The slave must give a zero-wait OKAY and ignore it.
  - `01` (BUSY): The master pauses *inside* a burst. Address and control already show the next beat. The slave must give a zero-wait OKAY and ignore it.
  - `10` (NONSEQ): A single transfer, or the *first* transfer of a burst.
  - `11` (SEQ): A later transfer in a burst, with the same control as the previous beat.
- `HSIZE [2:0]`: Bytes per transfer, `2^HSIZE`. It must not exceed the data bus width and stays constant through a burst (§3.4).
- `HBURST [2:0]`: Burst type (SINGLE, INCR, WRAP4, INCR4, …; table in section 4).

The decoder also drives one `HSELx` per slave. A slave samples `HSELx`, address and control **only when `HREADY` is high** (§4.2). While `HREADY` is low, whatever sits on the address bus has not been handed to anyone yet.

### Master $\leftrightarrow$ Slave (Data)
These signals carry the actual payload during the **Data Phase**:
- `HWDATA [31:0]`: Write data driven by Master.
- `HRDATA [31:0]`: Read data driven by Slave.

### Slave $\rightarrow$ Master (Response)
These signals are driven by the Slave during the **Data Phase**:
- `HREADYOUT`: High when the slave is finished with its data phase, Low when it needs more time (inserts wait states). The interconnect combines this into the global `HREADY` signal seen by all components.
- `HRESP`: High for ERROR, Low for OKAY.

## 2. Address/Data Phase Pipelining

Every AHB transfer consists of two distinct phases (§3.1):
1. **Address Phase:** Lasts one `HCLK` cycle *unless the previous transfer extends it*. The master drives address and control signals.
2. **Data Phase:** May take several cycles if the slave inserts wait states. Data is sampled at the rising edge where `HREADY` is high.

To maximize throughput, the Address phase of the *next* transfer overlaps with the Data phase of the *current* transfer.

## 3. Wait-State Insertion

Slaves are not always fast enough to process data in a single clock cycle. If a slave needs more time, it pulls its `HREADYOUT` signal low. This causes the global `HREADY` to drop, inserting a **Wait State**.

Crucially, because of the pipeline, stalling the Data phase of Transfer A *also stalls the Address phase of Transfer B* (§3.1, Figure 3-5).

Find the one cycle where `HREADY` is low: `HWDATA` still shows A there, and `HADDR` already shows B. Both are held for one more cycle.

### The Rules of `HREADY`
- The slave samples `HWDATA` (for writes) or the master samples `HRDATA` (for reads) only on the rising clock edge where `HREADY` is **High**. The master holds write data for the whole data phase (§6.1.1). A slave only has to drive valid read data in that final cycle (§6.1.2).
- Wait states use an OKAY response (§5.1.2). The spec recommends that slaves insert no more than 16 wait states (§5.1.2), but this is a recommendation, not a rule.
- A slave samples `HSELx`, address and control only when `HREADY` is high (§4.2). A held address phase is not a second transfer.
- While the transfer is waited, the master must keep `HTRANS` and the address stable, **with these exceptions** (§3.6.1, §3.6.2):
  - IDLE may change to NONSEQ, and the address of an IDLE may change;
  - in a fixed-length burst, BUSY may change to SEQ;
  - in an undefined-length INCR, BUSY may change to any type;
  - after an ERROR response, the master may switch to IDLE and change the address to cancel (see B-AHB-2).

  Once `HTRANS` becomes NONSEQ or SEQ, it must stay constant until `HREADY` is high.

## 4. Burst Types and Alignment

A burst is a sequence of transfers. Instead of negotiating for bus access for every single data word, a master can request a burst.

| `HBURST` | Type | Beats |
|---|---|---|
| `000` | SINGLE | 1 |
| `001` | INCR | undefined length |
| `010` / `100` / `110` | WRAP4 / WRAP8 / WRAP16 | 4 / 8 / 16 |
| `011` / `101` / `111` | INCR4 / INCR8 / INCR16 | 4 / 8 / 16 |

Encoding from §3.5, Table 3-3. The rules that matter for timing and checking:

- **NONSEQ, then SEQ.** The first beat is NONSEQ. Each later beat is SEQ, and its control matches the previous beat (§3.2).
- **Incrementing (INCR, INCRx):** Each address is the previous one plus the transfer size.
- **Wrapping (WRAPx):** The address wraps at a boundary of *beats × size* bytes (§3.5). A WRAP4 of words wraps at 16 bytes, so a burst from `0x34` visits `0x34`, `0x38`, `0x3C`, `0x30`. Wrapping bursts suit cache line fills.
- **BUSY between beats.** BUSY pauses a burst and already carries the next beat's address. A fixed-length burst must end with SEQ, not BUSY. A SINGLE must be followed by IDLE or NONSEQ, never BUSY (§3.5.1).
- **The 1KB rule.** Masters must not start an *incrementing* burst that crosses a 1KB address boundary (§3.5). The smallest region a slave can own is 1KB (§4.2), so the rule keeps every burst inside one slave. A WRAP burst never leaves its own *beats × size* block.
- **Alignment.** Every transfer in a burst is aligned to its size, for example `HADDR[1:0] = 2'b00` for words (§3.5).

## Summary

Pipelining and wait states are the beating heart of AHB timing. If you can trace exactly when `HREADY` is high, you know exactly when an address phase is accepted and when data is sampled.
